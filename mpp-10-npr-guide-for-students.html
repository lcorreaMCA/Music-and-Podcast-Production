Stop 1 of 8

New to Podcasting? Don't Panic

NPR's guide, "Starting Your Podcast: A Guide for Students," opens with a friendly message: NPR does not expect you to be an expert. Most students entering its challenge are making a podcast for the first time, and the contest is meant to be about learning new skills in a fun way.

The guide points out that podcasts come in all shapes and sizes, which gives you room to be creative. Some are long: This American Life episodes can run an hour or more. Others, such as WBUR's Kind World, last less than ten minutes. Some cover news, like NPR's daily Up First. Others tell stories, make people laugh, feature conversations, or teach a subject, like Hidden Brain (science and human behavior) and Planet Money (money and life).

Before you plan your own, the guide asks you to think about podcasts you already know. What keeps you listening? What bores you? Is there a host? Is it an interview, or does a narrator tell a story with interview clips in between? Answering those questions helps you choose a structure for your own show.

Most importantly, NPR challenges students to think about sound: to work with interviews, narration, and recorded sounds from the world around them, not just people talking into a microphone. This lesson follows the guide in order: tools, ideas, sound, interviews, scripts, and finally the contest rules.

Tools and Team

Do you need expensive gear? The guide's answer is no. NPR wrote it assuming you can make a podcast with two tools: a smartphone and a computer. If you carry a cellphone, you already have a recording device in your pocket, and used the right way it can record high-quality sound. You can use your phone's built-in voice recording app or another recording app.

Using a phone "the right way" means applying what you learned in Lessons 1 to 5. Hold the phone's microphone close to the person speaking and keep the distance steady; turn on airplane mode or silence notifications so calls and buzzes do not interrupt; record in a quiet room; and keep your hands still, because handling noise travels straight into a phone's tiny mic.

How many people do you need? The guide says there is no set number, but it helps to have a team with different roles. Common roles include a host or narrator who speaks to the audience, a reporter who gathers interviews and sounds, a producer who plans and keeps the project on track, and an editor who cuts and shapes the audio. NPR notes that what a producer does can vary from one show to another.

Your computer is where you edit. GarageBand and Audacity, which you used in Lessons 7 and 8, are both free and can do everything a student podcast needs: arranging clips, cutting, balancing levels, adding fades, and exporting.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Radio producers still call recorded audio "tape," even though almost nobody uses tape anymore.
Most phones contain more than one microphone; the main one is usually at the bottom edge near the charging port.

Finding Your Idea

NPR offers optional prompts, starting questions, to get you thinking. You do not have to use them, but they show the range of possible podcasts: tell a story about your school or community that your audience should know; describe a moment in history all students should learn about; show both sides of a debate about an issue important to you; explain what you want to change about the world; predict a big change students today will make in the future; or explain something that kids understand and grown-ups don't.

Before you settle on one idea, the guide suggests a quick brainstorm: try to come up with an idea for each prompt. While you brainstorm, think about structure too. Would this idea work best as an interview? As one host narrating a story with interview clips? What sounds would help listeners picture the story?

Then test the idea for feasibility, meaning whether you can actually do it. The guide warns that an idea requiring travel far away or an interview with someone famous might be difficult. Make sure you can reach the people and places your story needs before you commit. A story about your grandmother, your school's marching band, or a local bakery is far more doable than an interview with a movie star.

A good final check: can you say your idea in one sentence, with a clear question at its center? The NPR winners you studied in Lesson 9 all had one: why do I remember some moments and not others? What is it really like to have dyslexia? Why do people collect things?

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

The prompt "explain something kids understand and grown-ups don't" often produces funny, surprising podcasts about games, slang, or school life.
For a "both sides" podcast, interview at least one person from each side and let them speak in their own words.

Connection: Songwriting

Songwriters also start by brainstorming many ideas, then choosing the one with the strongest central "hook" or question. A podcast idea that fits in one sentence works like a song title that sums up the song.

Stop 4 of 8

Gathering Sound

NPR wants students to work with sound, so plan which sounds you will record before you go out. The guide points to a list from YR Media of sounds every podcaster should consider recording. Professionals call the natural sound of a place ambient sound (or "ambi," or "nat sound"): the hum of a cafeteria, birds in a park, a teacher's chalk on a board, a marching band warming up.

Ambient sound lets listeners "see" a place. Instead of saying "the band room was noisy," you can let listeners hear it for three seconds, then fade it under your narration. Record at least 30 seconds to a minute of each place, holding still and staying silent, so you have enough to work with when editing.

Prepare before you leave. The guide says to test your equipment first: do not get stuck in an interview with a phone that has no storage left or a microphone with dead batteries. Bring a teammate along to help when you go to an interview or record sounds.

After recording, the guide gives two habits that save hours later: listen to the audio soon after you record it, while you still remember what happened, and save and label it clearly so you know exactly what each file contains. NPR also offers a downloadable field recording checklist to take with you.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Recording outside a studio is called field recording. Wind is the biggest enemy, so shield the mic with your body or a foam cover.
Record a quiet moment in every interview location. Editors use it to smooth over cuts.

Connection: Film sound

Film crews record "wild sound" and room tone at every location so editors can build a believable world around the dialogue. Podcasters use the same technique to place listeners in a scene.

Interviews the NPR Way

Many student podcasts include interviews, and the guide stresses making the most of the time you spend recording someone. Before you go: do your research, so you understand why you are interviewing this person; read up on who they are, what they do, and the subject. Prepare a list of open-ended questions, questions that cannot be answered with just "yes" or "no." It is fine to veer off course and ask follow-up questions you did not plan, but the list gets things started.

Clarify the terms of the interview. The person must understand that you are recording audio and what it is for, such as the NPR Student Podcast Challenge. This is a matter of fairness and ethics: people have the right to know when and why they are being recorded.

When you arrive, meet somewhere quiet, unless you want to record the noise. The guide specifically warns against a busy mall or a windy park. Indoors, head to a quiet room and close the door. If you interview someone at an event, some background noise is fine, as long as it is the sound you want listeners to hear.

During the interview, bring a teammate to keep time and note the interesting or compelling moments, writing down the time each one happens. Those notes will save you hours when you are editing and searching for the best moments later.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

After a guest answers, wait a second or two before your next question. People often add their most interesting thought into the silence.
Avoid saying "uh-huh" while a guest speaks; nod instead, so your voice does not overlap theirs in the recording.

    Scripts and Narration

    At NPR, some podcasts are scripted, meaning the order of everything is planned before the host records the narration. That planning happens only after the reporters have gathered their "tape," the sound from interviews and other recordings. In other words, you collect first, then write around your best material.

    The guide says there is no single correct way to write a script, but it helps to mark three things: where the narrator speaks, where you will use sounds or interview clips recorded earlier, and how long those clips last. NPR shares a sample script from its training team as a model. This is the same two-column thinking you practiced in Lesson 6.

    Even if you do not script the whole podcast, the guide recommends writing out at least one part: the very beginning. The first seconds decide whether listeners stay, so the opening deserves the most careful writing and rehearsal.

    Then record the narration. If you feel nervous about your voice, the guide offers encouragement: your voice is what makes you, and your podcast, sound unique. Aim to sound natural, alert, and energetic, and to sound like the real you. Everyone stumbles over words. When that happens at NPR, producers often go back to the script and write shorter sentences that are easier to read and will not leave the host out of breath.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Writing a clip's length in the script, such as "(0:18)," helps you see how long the whole podcast will be before you edit.
    Many narrators stand while recording, because it helps them breathe and sound more energetic.

    Connection: Theatre

    Actors rehearse their lines until they sound spontaneous. Narrators do the same: a script well rehearsed sounds less like reading and more like talking.

    The Rules and Submitting

    Every contest has rules, and breaking them gets an entry disqualified. NPR announces the rules each year, so always read the current official rules before you begin. For the 2026 challenge for grades 4 to 12, podcasts had to be between three and eight minutes long and the original work of students, created for the contest. Entries opened January 5, 2026, and closed May 31, 2026, at 11:59 p.m. Eastern time.

    Music is the rule that trips up the most students. NPR allows only original music, composed and performed by the students themselves, because using music from the web involves complicated legal issues. NPR even suggests asking your school's band to play an original composition. For a music production class, this is an opportunity: you can compose and record your own theme.

    Only a teacher, educator, or other adult who is 18 or older can fill out the submission form, but adults may not produce or edit the podcast. NPR asks students to keep the language clean. The official rules also require the podcast to be uploaded to an approved platform with downloading enabled.

    Fact check

    NPR's official rules list SoundCloud, Podbean, and "Anchor" as upload platforms. Anchor no longer exists under that name: Spotify renamed it Spotify for Podcasters in 2023 and Spotify for Creators in 2024. Check the current rules for the exact list of approved platforms.

    Prizes vary from contest to contest and year to year, but the biggest reward is that winning podcasts are featured on NPR programs. And the guide's real message is that the process itself is the prize: by entering, you learn the skills professional audio journalists use every day.
