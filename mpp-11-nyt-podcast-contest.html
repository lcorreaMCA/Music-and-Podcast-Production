The Learning Network's Contest

The New York Times runs a free website for teachers and students called The Learning Network. Each school year it hosts several student contests, including a podcast contest that invites teenagers to create an original podcast of five minutes or less that informs or entertains listeners. Its favorite entries are published on The Learning Network.

The contest is open to students ages 13 to 19 who are in middle school or high school, anywhere in the world. College students cannot enter, although students 19 or younger on a gap year can. Entering is free, and you do not need a New York Times subscription.

The 2026 contest was the ninth annual podcast contest. Entries were accepted from April 8 to May 13, 2026, and winners are typically announced a couple of months later. For the 2026–27 school year, The Learning Network's published calendar lists the podcast contest, now called "Audio Stories," from March 31 to May 12, 2027. Always confirm the dates and rules on the official contest page when it opens.

In the "Writing for Podcasts" webinar you heard in Lesson 6, Learning Network staffer Callie Holterman explains why the contest exists: students get to create something for a real audience, and the Times team listens to every entry before choosing winners, runners-up, and honorable mentions.

Learning Network contests usually close at 11:59 p.m. Pacific time, which is 1:59 a.m. the next morning in Louisiana.
The Learning Network also runs contests for personal essays, open letters, reviews, and STEM interview videos.

The Rules

Read the rules before you record. In past contests, the key rules have been: the podcast must be five minutes or less (the Learning Network once put it plainly: 5:01 is longer than five minutes); it must be original, made specifically for this contest and not already published elsewhere; and it must have a beginning, middle, and end.

You can choose any topic and any format or genre: an interview, a conversation with friends, a news investigation, a personal narrative, or a fictional story. Students may work together, but each student may be part of only one entry. Entries are uploaded to a podcasting platform of your choice (in 2025, the rules excluded Google Drive) and submitted with an online form.

The music rule: you cannot use copyrighted sound effects or music unless they fall under limited fair use exceptions (explained in Stop 6). The safest path is to compose your own music or use sounds you have permission to use.

For 2026–27, The Learning Network has announced stricter rules about generative AI, computer programs that create text, images, or audio on request. According to its announced rules, students may not use AI chatbots to draft their work, and many contests ask students to show their creative process, such as drafts and version history. Plan to keep your outline, script drafts, and raw recordings as evidence of your own work.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

A podcast already published on a radio station or another contest site is not eligible. A teacher's class page does not count as publishing.
If you submit as a team, include the names of everyone involved.

What the Judges Want

In the webinar, Callie Holterman describes what judges have in mind as they listen. A rubric is a list of the qualities used to judge work, and the podcast contest's comes down to four areas.

Content: the podcast should be interesting and have a point. It can inform or entertain, but listeners should leave knowing something new, or having explored a subject from a fresh perspective. Flow: even a short podcast can have a narrative arc, a beginning, middle, and end, so the listener is carried from start to finish.

Editing: judges are not looking for professional polish, but they do want to hear that students were thoughtful and intentional about how they used music, sound effects, and multiple voices to tell the story. Guidelines: follow every rule, because an entry that breaks one cannot be considered, however good it is.

Holterman also clears up a common worry: you do not need to tackle a gigantic topic to stand out. Winners have covered very personal and very narrow subjects as well as big issues, as long as the story genuinely matters to the student telling it.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

If you can explain why each sound is there, it is intentional. If you can't, cut it.
A familiar topic can still win if you bring a perspective listeners have not heard, such as a teenager's view of a community issue.

Connection: Music performance

A music competition judge listens for musical content, phrasing that flows, intentional expression, and correct repertoire rules. The podcast rubric asks for the same qualities in a different medium.

Choosing a Topic That Wins

The webinar describes the wide range of past winners. Some students told about living through the coronavirus pandemic. Some chose a narrow subject close to them, such as a podcast about graffiti art. Others took on a huge issue from a student's point of view, such as a podcast about their generation and the climate.

To find your topic, the Learning Network suggests its writing prompts, questions that get students thinking. Examples from the webinar include: Who do you think has been overlooked by history? Is it harder to grow up in the 21st century than in the past? What have you learned from animals? Are you the same person on social media as in real life? Writing a few sentences in answer to a prompt can reveal a podcast idea.

Remember the advice from student winners in the webinar. One student's dad told her that talking about something people her age care about is a podcast. Two winners said the most important thing was genuine interest and a feeling of authenticity throughout. Librarian Laura Winick adds that the experts for your story may be the people in your own home.

Finally, keep the scope small enough for five minutes. Winick stresses that first-time podcasters cannot manage hours of audio. Choose one question you can answer well in five minutes, rather than a whole subject you can only skim.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

A prompt answer that makes you want to keep talking is a sign you have found your topic.
A national issue becomes a strong student podcast when you show how it affects your own school or neighborhood.

Winning Moves

Two winning podcasts from the webinar show moves you can borrow. Daniel and Ian's How My Town Found the Universe, about the history of radio astronomy in Holmdel, New Jersey, opens with an old-time radio clip that sets a mysterious, historical mood before listeners even know the topic. Laura Winick says that mystery is what hooks you.

Their podcast also includes an interview with a real expert, radio astronomer Robert Woodrow Wilson, who lives in their town. Daniel and Ian said they first decided on their theme and message, then planned what each segment needed to accomplish at the beginning, middle, and end. Combining old radio clips, narration, and the interview, they wanted to take listeners on a journey.

Ari Gibson's conversation podcast compared how dating has changed between her parents' generation and her own, using an interview with her parents. Her strength was chemistry and a strong outro: she previewed future episodes and invited listeners to think about the question themselves, which made the show sound professional and complete.

The patterns: a hook that creates curiosity, a clear plan for each segment, real voices from people who know the story, sound used with purpose, and an ending that leaves the listener thinking. These are the same patterns NPR's winners showed in Lesson 9.

Copyright and Fair Use

Copyright is the legal right of creators to control how their work is copied and used. Almost every song, recording, and sound effect you find online is copyrighted by someone. Using it without permission can get your entry disqualified, and on platforms like YouTube, can get your audio removed.

Fair use is a limited exception in United States law that allows using small parts of copyrighted work without permission for purposes such as commentary, criticism, teaching, or news reporting. Courts weigh several factors, including the purpose of the use, how much is used, and whether it harms the market for the original. Fair use is decided case by case; it is not a simple rule like "under ten seconds is always fine," which is a common myth.

Because fair use is complicated, the safest choices are: compose and record your own music, which this course has prepared you to do; record your own sound effects, like knocking on a table to make a door knock, as student winner Daniel suggested; or use royalty-free or Creative Commons audio whose license clearly allows your use, and credit it as the license requires.

Note that the two contests treat this differently. The New York Times allows copyrighted material only under fair use exceptions, while NPR allows only music composed and performed by the students. If you plan to enter both, follow the stricter NPR rule and use original music.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Works whose copyright has expired enter the public domain. In the U.S., compositions published before 1931 are now in the public domain, but a modern recording of them may still be copyrighted.
Writing "music by..." in your credits does not give you the right to use copyrighted music.

Connection: Visual art

Collage artists and photographers face the same questions about copying and transforming other people's images. Every art form balances inspiration with respect for creators' rights.

NYT and NPR Side by Side

The New York Times contest and NPR's Student Podcast Challenge share a goal: a real audience for student voices. But their rules differ, so compare them before you decide where to submit, or prepare one podcast that fits both.

Length: the Times allows five minutes or less, while NPR requires three to eight minutes. A podcast between three and five minutes fits both. Who can enter: the Times is open to ages 13 to 19 in middle or high school worldwide; NPR is for grades four through 12 in the United States. Who submits: Times entrants submit with the online form, while NPR requires a teacher or other adult 18 or older to submit.

Music: the Times bars copyrighted music and sound effects unless they fall under fair use; NPR allows only original music made by the students. Timing: the Times contest runs in the spring (March 31 to May 12, 2027), while NPR's 2026 challenge accepted entries from January to the end of May. Audience: Times winners are published on The Learning Network; NPR winners are featured on national radio programs.

The smart plan: make a podcast between three and five minutes, using only original music and your own sound effects, with a clear beginning, middle, and end. That one podcast can meet the rules of both contests. Always check the newest official rules, because both organizations update them each year.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Aim for about four and a half minutes. That leaves a safety margin under the Times limit and comfortably over NPR's minimum.
Before submitting anywhere, reread that contest's rules on the day you submit.
