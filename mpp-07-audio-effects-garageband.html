What an Effect Does

An audio effect is a process that changes a sound. Some effects fix problems, such as removing rumble or evening out a voice that is sometimes loud and sometimes quiet. Others add creative color, such as the sound of a large hall or a repeating echo. In a DAW like GarageBand, effects come as plug-ins: small pieces of software you add, or "plug in," to a track.

In GarageBand for Mac, select a track and open the Smart Controls pane (press the B key). It shows simple knobs for the track's sound plus a Plug-ins area with slots. An empty Audio track in a new project usually starts with two plug-ins already loaded: a Compressor and a Channel EQ. You can click an empty slot to add more, drag plug-ins to change their order, and click a plug-in's power button to turn it off for comparison.

Most plug-ins come with presets, saved settings with names like "Vocal" or "Bright," which are good starting points. GarageBand's Library also has patches for the Voice category, such as narration settings for podcasts, that load a whole chain of effects at once. Presets are a start, not an answer: always listen and adjust.

Effects can be added in two main ways. An insert effect is placed directly on one track, so all of that track's sound passes through it; EQ and compression are usually inserts. A send effect lives in one shared place, and each track sends a chosen amount of its sound to it. GarageBand's Master Echo and Master Reverb work this way: every track can use the same reverb at different amounts, which helps all the sounds feel like they are in one space.

Here is the most important habit with any effect: use bypass, turning the effect off and on, to compare. If the track does not sound clearly better with the effect on, at the same volume, turn it off.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Many of GarageBand's plug-ins share their technology with Apple's professional DAW, Logic Pro.
Effects work in order from top to bottom, so EQ before compression can sound different from compression before EQ.

Connection: Photography

Photo editing apps have filters and sliders for brightness, contrast, and color. Audio effects do the same job for sound: subtle changes improve the picture, but too much makes it look fake.

EQ: Shaping the Tone

Equalization, or EQ, boosts or cuts specific frequency ranges. The effects textbook you shared calls EQ to engineers what screwdrivers are to mechanics: a basic tool used on almost every job. If a track is too boomy, a cut around 100 Hz can help; if it is missing warmth, a gentle boost around 300 Hz can add it.

There are several kinds of EQ. A graphic EQ has a row of sliders, each controlling a fixed frequency band. A parametric EQ lets you choose the exact frequency, how much to boost or cut, and how wide the affected range is, called the Q or bandwidth. A shelving EQ raises or lowers everything above or below a point, like a shelf. A filter removes frequencies on one side of a point: a high-pass filter, also called a low-cut, lets highs pass and removes lows. GarageBand's Channel EQ combines several of these in one graph you can drag with the mouse.

Useful starting points for a speaking voice: a high-pass filter around 80 to 100 Hz removes rumble and handling noise without thinning the voice; a small cut in the low-middle range (around 200 to 400 Hz) can reduce muddiness; and a gentle boost around 2 to 5 kHz adds presence, making words easier to understand. Every voice and mic is different, so treat these numbers as places to start listening.

To find a problem frequency, use the sweeping technique from the textbook: create a narrow boost, slowly sweep it across the frequencies until the problem sound jumps out, then turn that boost into a cut. The textbook also suggests imagining the result of a boost or cut before you make it, which trains your ears. In general, cutting is gentler than boosting, and small moves of 2 to 4 dB are often enough.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

In a mix, EQ helps instruments share space: if the bass and kick fight, cut a little of one where the other is strongest.
Every musical note contains a fundamental pitch plus higher harmonics. EQ on the harmonics changes the instrument's tone color, or timbre.

Compression: Evening Out the Volume

A compressor automatically turns down sounds that get louder than a level you choose, which narrows the difference between the loud and quiet parts. That difference is called dynamic range. Compression makes a voice sit steadily in a mix, so listeners do not have to keep reaching for the volume knob.

The threshold sets the level where compression starts: anything louder than the threshold gets turned down. The ratio sets how strongly: at 4:1, a sound that goes 4 dB over the threshold comes out only 1 dB over. Attack is how quickly the compressor reacts once a sound crosses the threshold, and release is how quickly it lets go. Because compression lowers the loud parts, compressors also have a gain or makeup control to bring the whole track back up.

The textbook advises moderate threshold and ratio settings, which make a track denser and warmer without crushing its dynamics. Longer attack and release times make the compressor less noticeable and prevent pumping, an unnatural "breathing" of the volume. A very fast attack can flatten the sharp start of drum hits; a slower attack lets that punch through.

The most common beginner mistake is to compress everything heavily. Over-compressed tracks sound flat, tiring, and lifeless, and they exaggerate breaths and room noise. Listen with bypass: the compressed version should sound more even and present, not squashed.

Fact check

The MakeUseOf glossary you shared says a compressor "increases the volume again when the amplitude drops below another set level." That is not how a compressor works. A compressor only turns down sounds above the threshold; the whole track is then raised with makeup gain. The same glossary calls parallel compression "a type of multi-band compression." It is not: parallel compression blends a heavily compressed copy of a track with the original, uncompressed track.

Limiters, Gates, and De-essers

A limiter is an extreme compressor. The textbook describes it as having the highest ratio possible (infinity to one) and an extremely short attack: no sound is allowed past its ceiling, no matter what. Engineers place a limiter at the very end of a mix or podcast to prevent clipping and raise the overall level safely. Podcasting Pro Basics calls this step "hard limiting."

A noise gate works the opposite way: it silences a track whenever the sound drops below its threshold. That removes background hum, hiss, or headphone bleed in the pauses between phrases. The textbook advises setting the threshold just high enough to mute the background noise while leaving the music or speech intact. Set it too high and the gate chops off the quiet beginnings and endings of words.

A de-esser targets only the sharp, hissing "s," "sh," and "ch" sounds called sibilance. It turns down that narrow high-frequency range only at the moments sibilance occurs, so the rest of the voice keeps its brightness. De-essers are especially useful after you add a presence boost with EQ, which can make sibilance stronger.

Together these tools solve the most common voice problems: the limiter controls the loudest peaks, the gate cleans up the silences, and the de-esser tames harsh consonants. Each one should work quietly in the background; if you can clearly hear it working, it is probably set too strongly.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Many podcasters set their final limiter ceiling around −1 dB so the file never clips after it is converted to MP3.
Engineers often gate tom-tom drum mics so they only open when the tom is hit, reducing bleed from the rest of the kit.

Connection: Theatre sound

Live theatre mixers use gates and compressors on actors' body mics so a whisper and a shout both reach the back row clearly, without feedback.

    Reverb and Delay: Creating Space

    The textbook explains the difference between two related effects. Reverb is made of many sound reflections blended so closely with the original sound that we hear them as the sound of a room. Delay, or echo, is made of distinct reflections we hear as separate repeats. Both make a dry recording sound like it exists in a real space.

    Reverb has a few key settings. Reverb time (or decay) is how long the sound lingers: the textbook gives about half a second for a bedroom and around one and a half seconds or more for a concert hall. Pre-delay is the short gap before the first reflection arrives; a little pre-delay keeps the voice clear and in front while the reverb blooms behind it. Diffusion simulates how rough the room's surfaces are; rougher surfaces give a smoother, warmer reverb.

    Delay's main setting is the delay time between repeats, which usually sounds best when it matches the song's beat, for example a repeat every quarter note. Feedback sets how many repeats you hear before the echo fades out. In GarageBand, Master Echo and Master Reverb are shared send effects; turn up each track's amount in its Smart Controls.

    The textbook warns that reverb is among the most misused effects: too much turns a mix into "a muddy or boomy mess," a typical beginner mistake. Cut the low frequencies from the reverb to avoid mud. Use reverb and delay to create depth: less on vocals and solo instruments to keep them in front, more on instruments meant to sit farther back. For a spoken podcast, most hosts use little or no reverb, because a dry, close voice sounds intimate and clear.

    Fact check

    The MakeUseOf glossary calls early reflections "a continuation of a reverb tail." It is the other way around: early reflections are the first echoes to arrive, bouncing off the nearest surfaces, and the smooth reverb "tail" comes after them.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Some reverbs load a recording of a real space, called an impulse response, so your track can sound like it was played in a famous hall or cathedral.
    A single fast echo of about 100 milliseconds, called slapback, gave early rock-and-roll vocals their famous sound.

    Connection: Architecture

    Concert halls are designed for a specific reverb time. A digital reverb lets an engineer "move" a performance into a cathedral, a gym, or a tiny room without leaving the studio.

    Color and Movement Effects

    Some effects add movement. Chorus makes one sound seem like several, like a choir instead of one singer. It works by copying the sound and delaying the copy by a slowly changing amount, which also shifts its pitch slightly. The textbook suggests using it subtly on background parts such as backing vocals, strings, pads, and rhythm guitars.

    Chorus has relatives called modulation effects. A flanger uses an even shorter changing delay, creating a sweeping "jet plane" sound. A phaser creates a similar swirling sweep in a different way. Tremolo raises and lowers the volume in a steady rhythm, creating a wobble. These effects became popular in the psychedelic music of the 1960s and are still used today, usually more gently.

    Other effects add color or grit. Distortion deliberately overloads a signal, adding extra harmonics that make the sound fuzzy, aggressive, and full; it is the core sound of rock guitar. Gentler saturation imitates the warm, slight distortion of tape machines and tubes. GarageBand also includes amp simulators that make a guitar plugged straight into the computer sound like it is playing through a real amplifier, and on iPad and iPhone a Vocal Transformer that shifts a voice's pitch, which can be fun for characters in audio fiction.

    Color and movement effects are mostly creative choices, not fixes. Ask what feeling the effect adds to the song or story. A dreamy chorus on a background guitar might fit a slow song; the same chorus on a podcast host's voice would just sound strange.

    Fact check

    The MakeUseOf glossary describes distortion as a process where "audio is enhanced." Distortion actually changes the shape of the sound wave by overloading it, which adds new harmonics. It can sound exciting on a guitar, but it is not an improvement to every sound; unwanted distortion from clipping is a serious recording problem.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Before digital effects, organists used a spinning speaker called a Leslie to create a swirling, chorus-like sound.
    A "bitcrusher" effect lowers the bit depth and sample rate on purpose to create a gritty, retro video-game sound.

    Connection: Visual art

    Painters use texture, from smooth glazes to rough brushstrokes, to set a mood. Distortion and saturation are textures for sound, and chorus is like layering translucent paint.

    Building an Effects Chain

    A series of effects on one track is called an effects chain. A common starting chain for a spoken voice in GarageBand goes in this order: (1) a noise gate, if needed, to clean up the pauses; (2) EQ to cut rumble and mud; (3) a compressor to even out the level; (4) a de-esser, if the "s" sounds are sharp; and, for music, (5) a send to reverb or delay for space. A limiter goes last, on the master track.

    Make decisions while listening in context. A vocal can sound perfect alone but get lost when the music plays, so make most EQ, compression, and reverb choices with the whole mix playing. Solo a track only to check for technical problems like clicks or noise. This matches the Berklee "focus approach" to mixing: set a rough balance, then adjust whatever sounds wrong in context.

    Compare fairly. An effect that makes a track louder will almost always seem "better," even when it is not. Before you decide, match the volume with and without the effect, then use bypass. Check your work on several systems: headphones, a phone speaker, earbuds, and a car if you can.

    Finally, remember the rule from Lesson 3: effects cannot rescue a bad recording. A clean, well-placed microphone in a quiet room needs only small, gentle effects. When you notice yourself adding effect after effect to fix a track, it is often faster to record it again.

    Fact check

    The MakeUseOf GarageBand guide tells Mac users to press "Ctrl + A" to select all notes. On a Mac the shortcut is Command (⌘) + A.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    GarageBand lets you save a track's settings as your own patch, so your podcast voice chain is ready for every episode.
    Many professional engineers say their best mixes used fewer plug-ins than their worst ones.
