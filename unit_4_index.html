      Introduction to Songwriting

      Learn what a song is made of, how to catch ideas before they disappear, and how to start writing about what matters to you.

      What a song is made of

      PartWhat it is
      ChordsTwo or more tones played together in harmony
      MelodyA sequence of single tones: the part you sing
      RhythmThe song’s beat or pulse
      LyricsThe words

      Most successful songwriters are strongest in one of these areas. Very few people do it all, and even writers who can often team up with others. A great song frequently comes from a blend of different styles and personalities.

      Catch your ideas

      Ideas show up anywhere: in the car, in the shower, even in dreams. Paul McCartney has said some of his best songs came to him while he was asleep.

      When a melody or a line pops into your head, capture it right away. Your phone is always with you: type the words in a notes app or hum the tune into a voice memo. Don’t tell yourself you’ll remember it later. Even the ideas that feel unforgettable fade fast, and some great songs were never finished because the writer couldn’t get that first spark back.

      Write what you know and feel

      Your way of seeing the world is your best source of material. The moods you live with will show up in your songs, so write about what you actually know and feel, and your song will sound like you. When listeners recognize a bit of themselves in your words, the song stays with them.

      When music and lyrics match (or don’t)

      In many great songs, the music and the words fit each other perfectly. Listen to the difference between a major and a minor chord:

      • Minor chords often sit under sadder, deeper, more inward-looking songs.
      • Major chords tend to sound happier and more hopeful.
      • Sometimes the mood of the lyrics contrasts with the music. That bittersweet clash can make a song even more powerful.

      Tips to start composing

      1. Find a message you care about. Write about what interests you and what you know. If a subject matters to you, it may matter to others too. If you don’t care about it, your listeners won’t either.
      2. Keep it simple. The easiest melodies are often the most memorable.
      3. Find a simple set of chords. Explore your keyboard or guitar until you find a few that fit your idea.
      4. Find a place to write. Somewhere quiet where you can clear your head.
      5. Share your work. Feedback is how songs get better. Take in the praise and think about the criticism. Don’t ignore either one. Before you change anything, ask yourself what feels true to your song.

      Song Concepts and Object Writing

      Find a clear idea for your song, then use a 10-minute sensory freewrite to collect vivid images you can turn into lyrics.

      Start with a concept

      Before you write lyrics, it helps to have a concept: a roadmap that keeps you headed toward a finished song.

      • Write one sentence that explains what your song is about. That sentence is your song’s thesis.
      • Check back often. Do your new lines still support that sentence?
      • If the lyrics pull somewhere else, you may need a new thesis, or you may have two different songs.
      • One song, one idea. Every line should support it. When two ideas compete, neither one works.

      Writing with a partner? Start by asking each other what matters most in your lives and what you care about. Keep talking until you find an idea that feels real to both of you.

      Subjects that have inspired songwriters forever

      • Love
      • Friendship
      • Family
      • Conflict
      • Loss
      • Music and song
      • Towns and travel
      • Faith, hope, and belief
      • Motivation and sports
      • Past, present, and future
      • States of mind

      The subjects are broad. What makes your song unique is the situation you create and the feeling you bring to it.

      What is object writing?

      Object writing is a 10-minute freewrite that focuses only on sensory details. Instead of writing about your thoughts or opinions, you describe what you can see, hear, smell, taste, and touch. Berklee College of Music professor Pat Pattison made this exercise popular, and songwriters around the world use it.

      Why the senses?

      Senses are closely tied to memory. A smell can send you straight back to a moment from your childhood. When you practice writing with sensory detail, you open a door to memories and feelings you can use in songs.

      Example: the kitchen table

      The wooden surface feels rough under my fingertips, tiny scratches catching my nail as I drag it across. Coffee rings stain the oak in overlapping circles, some dark brown, others faded to pale yellow. The morning light streams through the window, casting long shadows from the salt and pepper shakers. I can smell yesterday’s garlic still lingering, mixed with the fresh scent of oranges in the fruit bowl. The table creaks when I lean my elbows on it, a familiar sound that echoes through the empty house. Crumbs from this morning’s toast scatter across the surface, rough against my palm when I brush them away. The wood grain runs in long, wavy lines like rivers viewed from an airplane. A sticky spot near the edge catches my sleeve, probably honey from yesterday’s tea. The chairs scrape against the linoleum when I pull them out, metal legs against worn flooring. Sunlight warms the surface where it hits, while the shadowed areas remain cool to the touch. I can taste the dust motes floating in the light beam, dry and metallic on my tongue.

      Why object writing works

      • It builds descriptive language that makes lyrics vivid and memorable.
      • Sensory triggers reach memories you wouldn’t think of on purpose.
      • It produces surprising images and metaphors.
      • It builds stamina and proves you always have something to write about.
      • It breaks perfectionism, because the process matters more than the product.
      • It gives you raw material to mine for songs later.

      Turning object writing into lyrics

      1. Look for the emotion hiding under your sensory details.
      2. Find the universal experience inside the specific details.
      3. Find the story the details are telling.
      4. Pull out your strongest images to use as lyric hooks.

      From the kitchen table freewrite, you might pull out:

      Coffee rings like memories overlapping

      Shadows dancing from the salt and pepper shakers

      Honey-sticky moments that catch my sleeve

      Advanced object writing techniques
      • Time travel: choose an object from your past, like a childhood toy, and describe it in the present tense. This often unlocks strong memories.
      • Seasons: write about the same object in different seasons. A park bench in July and a park bench in January are two different worlds.
      • Perspective shifts: write about the object as a little kid, as an adult, or as someone from another time or place.
      • Destination writing: songwriter Andrea Stolpe uses a version of this exercise that starts with a place, person, or time instead of an object, like “motel room” or “last day of summer.” It often leads straight to a song’s central idea.

      Object Writing Practice Challenge

      1. Choose a simple object: a coffee mug, a pencil, an apple, an old photograph, car keys.
      2. Start the timer and write continuously about it using only your five senses.
      3. Keep your pen moving, even if you repeat words.
      4. Avoid emotions and opinions. Stick to sensory details.
      5. Don’t worry about grammar or spelling. This is about flow, not perfection.

      No Rhyme Songs

      Write a song with no rhymes, so your story, images, and feelings have to carry it.

      Why write without rhyme?

      Without a rhyme scheme to lean on, you have to focus on storytelling, imagery, and emotional connection. This exercise shows whether your song has substance beyond clever wordplay, and it helps you find the natural rhythm of how you speak.

      A long tradition

      Unrhymed songs have deep roots in folk music around the world. Old ballads often put the story ahead of rhyme. In modern music, artists like Leonard Cohen, Bob Dylan, and Sufjan Stevens have written powerful songs with little or no rhyme.

      Without rhyme, these tools do the work

      • Steady rhythm: matching lines have about the same number of syllables.
      • Repetition: a key word or phrase comes back, especially in the chorus.
      • Parallel structure: lines built the same way (“I left the… / I left the…”).
      • Strong imagery: concrete details from your object writing.

      Example

      Verse 1

      Walking through the empty hallway after school

      Doors slam shut like thunder in the distance

      My backpack weighs heavy with unfinished grades

      The cleaning lady nods as she mops the floor

      Her phone plays something soft and forgotten

      While the fluorescent lights buzz overhead

      Verse 2

      Outside, the cars are pulling away

      Kids laughing in Cub Corner on the first floor

      My phone buzzes with messages from friends

      Making plans I can’t join

      The parking lot empties one car at a time

      Until only mine remains

      Chorus

      Driving home after too many bad grades

      Past houses where families gather together

      Kitchen windows glowing warm and yellow

      I wonder what they talk about at home

      If they talk about their kids’ bad grades

      Or if they sit quiet, in silence

      Notice the object-writing details: slamming doors, buzzing lights, glowing yellow windows. They do the work rhyme would usually do.

      Advanced no-rhyme techniques
      • Stream of consciousness: write your thoughts exactly as they come, without editing for logic. It can sound very honest.
      • Dialogue: include real conversations or overheard bits to add realism and character.
      • Time jumping: move between different times in the same song to show how experiences connect.
      • Multiple perspectives: tell the same story from different points of view.

      Listen: famous songs without rhyme

      No Rhyme Song Challenge

      Your song needs at least a verse, a chorus, and a bridge.

      1. Choose one specific story or idea that happened recently. Don’t try to cover too much ground.
      2. Write like you’re telling a friend about it. Use natural speech patterns.
      3. Paint the scene with vivid sensory details (use your object writing skills).
      4. Focus on the emotional journey, not clever wordplay.
      5. Don’t force rhymes. If one happens naturally, that’s fine, but don’t chase it.
      6. Read your lyrics out loud to make sure they flow when spoken.

      Music Theory Toolkit: Intervals, Scales, and Triads

      Understand how melodies come from scales and how chords are built from the same scale, so you can use both in your songs. Come back to this page whenever you need it.

      Try it on the keyboard: tap any two keys to hear them and see the distance between them.

      Tap a first note.

      Part 1: Half steps and whole steps

      A half step (also called a semitone) is the smallest distance between two notes in Western music. On a piano, it’s the very next key, black or white. A whole step (a tone) is two half steps.

      • E–F and B–C are half steps with no black key between them.
      • A sharp (♯) raises a note a half step. A flat (♭) lowers it a half step.
      • One key can have two names, like C♯ and D♭. These are enharmonic equivalents.

      In GarageBand’s piano roll

      Each row in the piano roll is one half step. Move a note up one row and it goes up a semitone; move it up two rows for a whole step. Twelve rows up brings you to the same note an octave higher.

      Part 2: Intervals

      An interval is the distance between two notes. Its name has two parts.

      Number: count the letter names from bottom to top, including both. C to G is C-D-E-F-G, so it’s a 5th. Sharps and flats don’t change the number.

      Quality: compare the top note to the major scale of the bottom note. Unisons, 4ths, 5ths, and octaves are perfect. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths, and 7ths are major. Make a major interval a half step smaller and it’s minor. A half step larger than major or perfect is augmented. A half step smaller than minor or perfect is diminished.

      Consonance and dissonance

      Octaves, 5ths, 3rds, and 6ths sound stable (consonant). The 4th can sound stable or tense depending on context. 2nds, 7ths, and the tritone sound tense (dissonant) and want to resolve. Dissonance isn’t “bad.” Tension and release is what makes music exciting, and songwriters use it on purpose.

      Why “perfect”? Musicians give different reasons. Some point to their very stable sound. Others note that perfect intervals stay perfect when you flip them upside down (invert them).

      Producer tip: a root plus a perfect 5th (7 rows up in the piano roll) is a power chord, the backbone of a lot of rock and electronic music.

      Part 3: Major scales

      Every major scale follows the same pattern of steps:

      W W H W W W H

      Rules: 8 notes, the same starting and ending note, every letter name used once, and no double sharps or double flats.

      1. Write the letters from G to G: G A B C D E F G.
      2. Write the pattern underneath the gaps.
      3. Add sharps where needed to fit the pattern: G A B C D E F♯ G.

      A common mistake is skipping the first whole step. If your scale ends on a different note than it started on, check your steps again.

      Try it: build D major yourself. (Check: it has two sharps.)

      Part 4: Minor scales
      • Natural minor pattern: W H W W H W W.
      • Relative minor shares the key signature of a major key. Start on the 6th note of the major scale (or go down 3 half steps). A minor is the relative minor of C major; E minor is the relative minor of G major.
      • Parallel minor starts on the same note as the major scale. Lower the 3rd, 6th, and 7th by a half step. C major becomes C minor: C D E♭ F G A♭ B♭ C.
      • Harmonic minor raises the 7th a half step, so it pulls strongly back home.
      • Melodic minor raises the 6th and 7th going up, and goes back to natural minor coming down.

      Major often sounds bright and minor often sounds darker, but listen and decide for yourself.

      Part 5: Triads

      A triad is a three-note chord built by stacking thirds: root, 3rd, and 5th. In root position on the staff, the notes sit all on lines or all in spaces, like a snowman.

      QualityBuilt fromOn CWritten
      MajorM3 + P5C E GC
      Minorm3 + P5C E♭ GCm
      Diminishedm3 + dim 5thC E♭ G♭C° or Cdim
      AugmentedM3 + aug 5thC E G♯C+ or Caug

      To build any triad, start with the major version, then adjust the 3rd and 5th. Inversions use the same notes with a different one on the bottom: 3rd on the bottom is first inversion, 5th on the bottom is second inversion. Inversions help chords connect smoothly.

      Try it: tap C, E, and G on the keyboard. Then change E to E♭. What changed in the sound?

      Capital Roman numerals are major chords, lowercase are minor, and ° is diminished. In minor keys, songwriters often make v major (V, E in A minor) for a stronger pull home.

      Part 7: How chords move
      • Tonic chords (I, plus vi and iii) sound like home.
      • Subdominant chords (IV, ii) move away from home.
      • Dominant chords (V, vii°) create tension that pulls back to I. They contain the leading tone (“ti”), which wants to rise a half step to “do.”

      Cadences (phrase endings)

      For each section of your song: should it end finished, unfinished, or surprising?

      Song spotlight: “Hallelujah” by Leonard Cohen

      In C major, the first verse moves through I and vi (C and Am), then IV, V, and back to I (F, G, C). Near the end it surprises you: an E major chord (the V of A minor) pulls the line to land on Am instead of home. What makes this song famous among songwriters is that the words of the first verse describe the very chord moves you’re hearing as they happen. Listen for it, and see if you can follow the Roman numerals.

      Part 8: Building melodies
      1. Pick a key and write out its scale. Those are your melody notes.
      2. Move mostly by step, with a few leaps for emphasis. After a big leap, step back the other way.
      3. Put chord tones on strong beats. Use other scale notes as passing tones in between.
      4. Use tendency tones: “ti” pulls up to “do,” “fa” pulls down to “mi,” “re” pulls down to “do.”
      5. End finished phrases on “do” over the I chord. End phrases that should hang on scale degree 2 or 5 over V.
      6. Stay in a comfortable singing range, and make the chorus a little higher or more energetic than the verse.

      Sing or play each phrase. Does it sound finished or unfinished? Why?

      Two-Chord Songs

      Write a song over just two chords, so your melody, rhythm, and lyrics have to carry it.

      The power of limitation

      Using only one or two chords forces you to focus on melody, rhythm, and lyrics instead of complicated chord progressions. That limit often leads to more creative ideas everywhere else, and the results can be surprisingly powerful and memorable.

      Two-chord songs show up across music history, from old folk songs to modern pop hits. The blues tradition in particular shows how much emotion you can get from very few chord changes.

      Lead sheets and chord charts

      • A lead sheet has the melody written in notation, with chord symbols above it. Songs usually include the lyrics.
      • A chord chart shows only chord symbols, with no notation. For pop songs, it usually includes the lyrics.

      Here’s a short chord chart for a made-up two-chord song. The chord sits right above the syllable where it changes.

      Verse
Em                         C
Headlights sweep across the ceiling
Em                          C
Somebody’s radio two floors down

Chorus
C                Em
Waiting for the late bus
C                Em
Waiting for the late bus home

      Making two chords interesting

      Rhythm
      • Verse: slow, fingerpicked arpeggios (one chord note at a time)
      • Chorus: fast, driving strumming
      • Bridge: syncopated, off-beat accents
      Melody
      • Use different octaves for different sections.
      • Start each section on a different note.
      • Create call-and-response between vocal lines.
      • Add harmonies that highlight different chord tones.

      Melody shapes

      Songwriting professor Jimmy Kachulis describes melodies by their shape. Try a few over your two chords:

      ShapeWhat it does
      StationaryStays on one note, like a straight line
      Zig-zagDecorates one note with the notes just above and below
      AscendingStarts low and climbs
      DescendingStarts high and falls
      ArchGoes up, then comes down
      Inverted archGoes down, then comes back up

      Note length matters too. Verses often use shorter notes packed close together, like talking. Choruses often use longer notes with more space, which makes the words feel bigger and more dramatic.

      Dynamics
      • Soft verses with whispered vocals
      • Loud, passionate choruses
      • Instrumental breaks with just the chords
      • A cappella sections that spotlight the melody
      Texture
      • Add percussion in some sections only.
      • Switch instruments (acoustic vs. electric guitar).
      • Layer vocals to thicken the chorus.
      • Strip down to voice and one instrument for intimate moments.

      Listen: two-chord songs

      Class activity: Write a two-chord song

      1. Choose your two chords and practice the progression until it feels natural.
      2. Experiment with different rhythmic patterns.
      3. Create a strong melody that works over both chords and uses chord tones well.
      4. Use rhythm changes to create contrast between sections.
      5. Vary your melody for the verse, chorus, and bridge while keeping the same chords.
      6. Focus on storytelling in the lyrics, since the chords won’t carry the song.
      7. Experiment with different chord voicings (inversions) for subtle variety.

      AABA Song Form

      Write a song in AABA form, the classic structure behind many of America’s best-known songs.

      A8 bars
      A8 bars
      Bbridge
      A8 bars

      How AABA is different

      AABA dominated popular music from the 1920s through the 1950s and still influences songwriters today. Unlike a verse-chorus song, it uses:

      • A sections as verses, with the same melody and different lyrics
      • a B section (the bridge) with a contrasting melody
      • no chorus: instead, a refrain line returns in each A section
      • traditionally 32 bars, 8 bars per section

      Where it came from

      AABA grew out of Tin Pan Alley, the New York songwriting scene, and became the standard form for American popular songs. Composers like George Gershwin, Cole Porter, and Irving Berlin perfected it. It shaped jazz standards and early rock and roll, and it gives you an alternative to the verse-chorus form you hear everywhere today.

      Section by section

      SectionJob
      A1Sets up the main melody and introduces the refrain
      A2Same melody; develops the story and ends with the refrain
      BContrasting melody and harmony; a new perspective
      A3Original melody returns; resolves the story with the refrain

      Extended example

      The refrain, “I’m just looking for magic,” closes every A section.

      A1: Setting up the situation

      I’ve been searching for something real

      In a world that feels like plastic

      Every face I see looks the same

      Every conversation feels rehearsed

      Walking through these crowded streets

      But feeling so alone

      Everyone’s connected to their screens

      I’m just looking for magic

      A2: Developing the theme

      Coffee shops and crowded trains

      Everyone staring at their phones

      Conversations feel so empty

      Like we’re reading from a script

      Dating apps and social media

      Promise connection but deliver less

      Swiping left through all these faces

      I’m just looking for magic

      B: A new perspective

      Maybe magic isn’t something you find

      Hidden in some distant place

      Maybe it’s something you create

      In the small moments between heartbeats

      In the way strangers’ eyes meet

      In the silence we don’t need to fill

      In the courage to be vulnerable

      When the world demands perfection

      A3: Resolution

      Now I see it in your smile

      In the way you laugh at nothing

      In how you listen with your whole heart

      When I tell you my worst fears

      In the way you hold my hand

      Like it’s the most natural thing

      In this moment, right here, right now

      I’m just looking for magic

      StyleA sectionsB section
      Classic jazz| C | Am | Dm | G7 | C | Am | Dm G7 | C || Em | Am | Dm | G7 | Em | Am | Dm | G7 |
      Folk/country| G | G | C | G | G | G | D | G || C | C | G | G | C | C | G D | G |

      Notice how each B section starts on a different chord than its A sections. That’s the contrast.

      AABA variations
      • AABA + tag: add a short ending that repeats the refrain.
      • AABA + intro: begin with an instrumental intro using the A melody.
      • Double AABA: repeat the whole form with new lyrics.
      • Modified bridge: make the B section longer or shorter (12 or 16 bars instead of 8).

      Listen: AABA songs

      Common mistakes

      MistakeFix
      The B section sounds too much like the A sectionsUse different chords, a different melody range, and a different rhythm.
      The refrain is weak or forgettableMake it the strongest, most memorable line in the song. It’s your hook.
      The B section doesn’t connect to the storyUse B to give context, ask a question, or offer a different angle on the A sections.

      Class activity: Write a simple AABA song

      1. Start with your refrain line. It’s your song’s main message and emotional center.
      2. Write your first A section, building up to the refrain.
      3. Write a second A section that develops the story or emotion further.
      4. Write a contrasting B section that offers a new perspective, asks a question, or gives context.
      5. Return to A for a satisfying ending that resolves the story.
      6. Make sure the A and B melodies contrast while still sounding like one song.

      Verb Strengthening

      Revise your lyrics with strong, specific verbs that show action, paint pictures, and set the mood all at once.

      Why verbs matter

      Verbs are the engine of language. One good verb can carry action, emotion, imagery, and character at the same time. Strong verbs create movement, set the mood, and help listeners picture and feel what you’re describing. When you hear a vivid action word, you almost feel the motion yourself, which is why songs with strong verbs stick.

      The verb upgrade process

      1. Write your lyrics first without worrying about verbs. Just get the ideas down.
      2. Underline every verb, including helping and linking verbs.
      3. Spot the weak ones: was, had, went, got, did, made, said, looked, walked.
      4. Replace them with stronger verbs that add meaning and imagery.
      5. Remove adverbs that the new verb makes unnecessary.
      6. Read each line aloud to make sure it still flows.

      Weak vs. strong

      Weak verb + adverbStrong verbEffect

      talked loudlyshouted, bellowed, roared, proclaimedEmotion and volume
      looked angrilyglared, scowled, stared, piercedA specific emotion
      moved slowlycrawled, crept, drifted, meanderedA specific pace and mood
      fell down hardcrashed, plummeted, collapsed, tumbledDrama and impact

      made musiccomposed, crafted, woveShows the creative process

      Strong verbs by category

      Movement

      • Gentle: drift, float, glide, sway, wander
      • Aggressive: charge, storm, attack, invade, conquer
      • Precise: dart, slice, pierce, thread, weave
      • Chaotic: tumble, spiral, crash, shatter, explode

      Emotion

      • Joy: sparkle, dance, sing, celebrate, radiate
      • Sadness: crumble, wilt, dissolve, sink, fade
      • Anger: burn, rage, storm, seethe, explode
      • Love: embrace, cherish, adore, treasure

      Senses

      • Sight: gleam, shimmer, flash, glow, shadow
      • Sound: whisper, roar, echo, ring, silence
      • Touch: caress, scratch, burn, freeze, tingle
      • Taste and smell: savor, devour, inhale, breathe

      By genre

      • Folk/country, earthy and physical: plow, harvest, wander, settle, weather
      • Rock/pop, energetic: explode, ignite, shatter, soar, crash
      • R&B/soul, flowing: flow, melt, burn, breathe
      • Hip-hop, sharp and percussive: snap, pop, slice, drop, hit

      Verbs as instant metaphors

      LineWhat the verb suggests
      She avalanched into the roomOverwhelming force, unstoppable momentum
      His words slithered through the conversationSomething sneaky or dangerous
      The melody cascaded over the audienceFlowing beauty that surrounds you
      Time evaporated in her presenceTime vanishing like magic
      Memories crystallized in that momentSudden clarity that lasts

      Before and after

      Before: The rain fell heavily on the roof
      After: Rain hammered the tin roof
      Why: “hammered” is percussive, sounds like the rain, and creates rhythm. “Fell heavily” was weak and predictable.

      Before: Love was growing between them
      After: Love bloomed in the space between their words
      Why: “bloomed” is natural and beautiful, and adds an image. “Was growing” was vague.

      Advanced verb techniques

      Tense shifts

      Present tense makes scenes feel immediate. Past tense works for reflection and storytelling. Mixing them shows time and emotional distance:

      Yesterday you walked away (past: finished)

      Today I’m drowning in the silence (present: ongoing)

      Tomorrow I will learn to breathe again (future: hope)

      Repetition

      Repeating a strong verb creates rhythm and emphasis:

      She runs from the past, runs toward tomorrow

      Runs until her lungs burn with possibility

      Verb chains

      A string of verbs builds intensity:

      The storm gathered, swelled, erupted, then vanished

      Leaving only puddles and the smell of rain

      Common mistakes

      • Too fancy. Strong doesn’t mean complicated. “Run” beats “perambulate.”
      • Too much. If every verb is huge, none stand out. Save the most powerful ones for key moments.
      • Breaking the rhythm. Always sing the new line. A great word that doesn’t fit the beat doesn’t work. Check that the stressed syllable of your new verb still lands on a strong beat.
      Your songwriter’s toolkit
      • Rhyming dictionary: even for no-rhyme songs, it helps you avoid accidental rhymes.
      • Thesaurus: especially useful for verb strengthening.
      • Voice recorder: catch melody ideas during exercises.
      • Notebook: keep all your exercises in one place.
      • Chord apps and a metronome: experiment with progressions and rhythm.
      • GarageBand: record your progress and ideas.
      • Song analysis: study how professional songwriters use these techniques across genres and eras.

      Class activity: Verb strengthening

      Part 1: Upgrade your verbs

      1. Identify: review your lyrics and underline all the verbs.
      2. Select: pick at least one weak or generic verb per section (verse, chorus, and so on) to upgrade.
      3. Replace: choose a stronger verb that does several jobs at once: action and movement, imagery, mood or emotion, and insight into the character.

      Example: “She walked into the room” only tells us she wasn’t standing still. “She slithered into the room” adds snake-like imagery and tells us something about who she is.

      Part 2: Cut the qualifiers

      1. In your revised lyrics, underline every adjective and adverb.
      2. Try cutting each one. If a verb needs an adverb to work (“lunged aggressively”), the verb probably isn’t strong enough yet.
      3. Choose one strong verb instead of a weak verb plus a modifier. Saying “the dog barked loudly” actually makes “barked” sound quieter.

      Today: plan and write

      1. Write your one-sentence concept (Rotation 2).
      2. Choose a form and list your sections in order: intro, verse, chorus, bridge, outro. AABA works too (Rotation 5).
      3. Pull images from your object writing and ideas from your phone notes.
      4. Choose a key, write out its scale, and pick chords from that key (Theory Toolkit, Parts 3 and 6). Two chords is fine, or use more if you’re ready.
      5. Draft lyrics for every section with strong verbs and sensory details (Rotation 6). Rhyme is your choice.
      6. Write your chords above your lyrics as you go. This becomes your chord chart.

      Song Draft Project: Melody and Arrangement

      Give your lyrics a melody and arrange your song in GarageBand so each section sounds different.

      Build your melody

      1. Use the notes of your scale as your melody notes.
      2. Put chord tones on strong beats, especially when the chord changes.
      3. Pick a melody shape for each section: ascending, arch, zig-zag, and so on (Rotation 4).
      4. Keep verse notes shorter and closer together. Give the chorus longer notes and make it a little higher.
      5. Sing each line. Do the stressed syllables land on strong beats?
      6. End finished phrases on “do” over the I chord. End phrases that should hang on V.

      Full melody guide: Theory Toolkit, Part 8.

      Arrange it

      Arrangement means deciding what plays when. The same chords can feel completely different depending on rhythm, dynamics, and texture.

      ToolTry
      RhythmArpeggios in the verse, strumming or driving chords in the chorus
      DynamicsSoft verse, loud chorus, a quiet moment before the last chorus
      TextureAdd drums or a second instrument only in the chorus; strip down in the bridge
      Intro and outroUse your chords or a piece of the melody with no vocals

      Today in GarageBand

      1. Open a new session and set your tempo and key.
      2. Record your chord progression on one track (piano or guitar).
      3. Record your melody on a second track: sing it, or play it on the MIDI keyboard.
      4. Add at least one arrangement change between sections: a drum track, a new instrument, or a dynamics change.
      5. Save your GarageBand session!
