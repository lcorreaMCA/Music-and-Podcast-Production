Stop 1 of 9

The Assignment

Your final project is one complete, original podcast episode. It must meet these requirements: a length of three to five minutes; a clear beginning, middle, and end; a point listeners can hear in the first 30 seconds; at least one interview or second voice; at least one example of purposeful ambient or recorded sound; and an original music theme or bed that you compose and record yourself in GarageBand.

Why these rules? A length of three to five minutes fits both contests you studied: NPR requires three to eight minutes, and The New York Times allows five minutes or less. Original music meets NPR's strict music rule, which also satisfies the Times. Built this way, your project can be submitted to either contest without changes.

You will deliver four things: (1) the finished episode as an MP3; (2) your project folder, including the GarageBand project and raw audio; (3) your script or outline with drafts; and (4) a short process log, notes written as you work about what you did each class and what you decided. The process log matters because contests now ask students to show evidence of their own creative process.

You may work alone or with one partner. If you work with a partner, each person must have a clear role, both voices should be heard, and your process log should show who did what.

Stop 2 of 9

Choosing Your Topic

Start with the strategies from Lessons 5, 6, 9, 10, and 11. Look for the overlap between your passion and your experience. Use a prompt: a story about your school or community, a moment in history students should know, something kids understand and grown-ups don't, or who has been overlooked by history. Think about people you can actually reach: family, teachers, coaches, neighbors, musicians in your community.

Write a pitch: a short proposal that sells your idea. A strong pitch answers five questions in about five sentences. What is the question at the center? Who is the audience? What format will it use? Whose voices will we hear? What sounds will help listeners picture the story?

Then run the feasibility test from NPR's guide. Can you record the people and places you need within the next two weeks? Can you answer the question well in under five minutes? If the answer to either is no, narrow the topic. "The history of jazz" is too big; "how my grandfather learned trumpet in his high school band" is a five-minute story.

Finally, check for sensitivity and permission. Will anyone you interview be uncomfortable having their story shared? Are there details that should stay private? Choose a topic you can tell honestly and respectfully, and get permission from everyone whose voice you record.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

In a music production class, topics about music itself make natural podcasts: how a song was made, the history of an instrument, or a musician's first performance.
Musicians, church choir directors, and band teachers in your community often love to share their stories.

Stop 3 of 9

Outline and Script

Build a two-column outline (Lesson 6) with five to ten blocks: voice on the left, sound and music on the right. Plan a hook for the first 15 seconds, a short intro that states your point, two or three middle sections that each move the story forward, and an outro with a takeaway and sign-off.

Prepare your interview questions: write about ten open-ended questions, get feedback from a classmate, and trim them to the five or six that reach the heart of your topic. Plan follow-up questions such as "What happened next?" and "How did that feel?"

Remember NPR's order: gather your tape first, then write the narration around your best moments. Before you interview, draft only the hook and intro. After the interview, choose the strongest clips, note their lengths, and write the narration that connects them. Mark every clip and sound in brackets, such as [CLIP: Grandpa, 0:22] or [MUSIC: theme fades under].

Write for the ear: short sentences, everyday words, repeated key ideas, and signposts. Read every draft aloud and time it. At about 130 to 160 words per minute, a four-and-a-half-minute episode with two minutes of interview clips needs only about 300 to 400 words of narration. Save each draft with a new number (Script_v1, Script_v2) for your process log.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

If you know your clips total 2:10, subtract that from 4:30 to find how much time is left for narration and music.
Your strongest interview moment can be your hook: play it first, then introduce yourself.

Connection: Theatre

A director blocks a scene before rehearsing it. Your two-column outline is the blocking for your podcast: it shows where every voice and sound will "stand."

Stop 4 of 9

The Production Schedule

Professionals plan milestones, checkpoints with due dates, and work backward from the deadline. Your project uses eight milestones, each roughly one class period of work time: (1) pitch approved; (2) research and questions ready; (3) interview and ambient sound recorded; (4) original music composed and exported; (5) script written and timed; (6) narration recorded; (7) rough cut edited; and (8) final mix exported and submitted.

A rough cut is the first complete version of your episode, with every part in place but not yet polished. It is the version you will bring to peer review in Lesson 13. A rough cut that exists is far more useful than a perfect intro with nothing after it.

Use the OTOB mindset from Lesson 4: on time and on budget. Your budget is class time. If a milestone takes longer than planned, adjust the next one instead of falling behind on everything. Leave a buffer before the deadline, because something always takes longer than expected.

Write a process log entry at the end of every work session: the date, what you did, any decisions or problems, and what you will do next. Two or three sentences is enough. These entries become your evidence of process, and they help you remember why you made each choice.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Schedule interviews first, because they depend on other people's calendars. Music and scripting can happen around them.
Plan one extra session as a backup in case an interview falls through or a file is lost.

Stop 5 of 9

Recording Plan

Use this technical checklist from Lessons 1 to 4 and 10. Settings: 48 kHz, 24-bit, WAV files while recording. Microphone: a cardioid dynamic mic, or a phone held steady, about six inches to a foot from the mouth, slightly angled to avoid plosives, with a pop filter if you have one. Room: the quietest, softest space you can find; turn off fans and notifications.

Levels: set the gain while the speaker talks at their loudest, aiming for peaks around −12 to −6 dBFS, and never let the meter reach red. Headphones: wear them so you hear problems as they happen. Room tone: record 30 seconds of silence in every location. Slate: say the date and take number at the start of each recording.

For interviews, follow NPR's steps: test your equipment, explain the terms of the interview, meet somewhere quiet, and bring a teammate to time the conversation and note the best moments. For ambient sound, record at least 30 seconds to a minute of each place without talking.

Right after each session: listen back, save with a clear file name in your project folder's raw audio subfolder, and make a backup copy. Then write your process log entry.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

If your mic has a pattern switch, choose cardioid for a single voice and point its null at any noise.
For a remote guest, ask them to record their side on their own phone and send you the file.

Stop 6 of 9

Composing Your Original Music

Your podcast needs original music: at least a short theme, a recognizable piece of music that opens and closes the episode, and possibly a quiet music bed to play under narration or stingers, very short musical phrases that mark transitions between sections.

Compose in GarageBand. Create a new project and set the tempo, key, and time signature in the display at the top of the window. Record parts with a Software Instrument track using a MIDI keyboard or the musical typing keyboard (Command + K), or record a real instrument with a microphone. String players: a simple cello or violin motif makes a beautiful, distinctive theme.

GarageBand also includes Apple Loops, short pre-recorded musical patterns. Apple's license allows you to use them in your own music, but a podcast built only from loops is not really your composition, and NPR's rule asks for music composed and performed by students. The safest approach: write and play your own melody and main parts, and use loops only as support, such as a drum pattern under your melody.

Keep the music serving the story. A theme of about 10 to 20 seconds is plenty. Export the theme and any beds or stingers as separate audio files into your project's Assets folder. In the podcast mix, the music bed should sit well below the voice: if you have to strain to hear a word, the music is too loud.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

A short motif of three to five notes is easier to remember than a long melody, which is why many famous themes are so short.
Match the music to your story: a curious, light theme for an explainer; a warm, slow one for an oral history.

Connection: Film scoring

Film composers write a main theme, then reuse pieces of it as short cues and transitions throughout the movie. Your theme, bed, and stingers work the same way on a smaller scale.

Editing and Mixing Checklist

Follow Lesson 8's workflow in GarageBand. Organize: one named track per voice, plus tracks for music and ambient sound; switch the ruler to time. Arrange: hook, intro, middle sections, outro. Cut: trim dead air, remove mistakes on all voice tracks at once with Command + T, and close gaps so everything stays in sync.

Clean: remove some filler words and long pauses but keep natural rhythm; fill gaps with room tone; smooth joins with short fades. Be honest: never edit a guest's words to change what they meant. Balance: keep every voice at about the same level, and use volume automation so the music bed fades under speech.

Effects (Lesson 7): a high-pass filter around 80 to 100 Hz on voices, gentle compression to even out levels, a de-esser if "s" sounds are harsh, and little or no reverb on voices. Final polish: a limiter on the master track with its ceiling around −1 dB, and an overall loudness near −16 LUFS if your software can measure it.

Export: Share, then Export Song to Disk, as an MP3, saved to your Exports folder with a clear name. Check the length: it must be between 3:00 and 5:00. Then listen to the whole file on a different device, such as a phone with earbuds, before you call it a rough cut or a final mix.

    How Your Project Is Graded

    The project rubric combines what NPR and The New York Times judges look for, plus the skills from this course. It has five criteria, each worth 20 points, for a total of 100.

    1. Content and point (20): the episode has a clear point, stated early, and teaches or reveals something worth hearing. 2. Structure and flow (20): a hook, a clear beginning, middle, and end, and transitions that carry the listener. 3. Voices and sound (20): at least one interview or second voice and purposeful ambient or recorded sound, all clearly recorded with good mic technique.

    4. Editing, music, and mix (20): clean cuts, balanced levels, an original theme that fits the mood, music that never covers words, and an export between 3:00 and 5:00. 5. Process and professionalism (20): a complete project folder, script drafts, a process log, permissions from everyone recorded, and all milestones met.

    Notice what the rubric does not reward: expensive gear, a famous guest, or lots of effects. As NPR's judges say, passion for the story matters as much as production skill. A simple, honest, carefully edited episode can earn full points.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Score your own rough cut with the rubric before peer review; you will practice this in Lesson 13.
    An episode that earns high marks on all five criteria is ready to submit to NPR or the Times.
