Stop 1 of 8

Before You Edit

The Riverside GarageBand tutorial begins with a reminder from Lesson 3: it is easier to prevent bad audio than to fix it. A quiet room, good mic placement, and careful levels save hours of editing. Still, every recording needs some editing, and GarageBand is a free, capable tool for it on a Mac.

Before you open GarageBand, gather three things: headphones, so you can hear small details; a separate audio track for every person in the recording; and your pre-made intro and outro. Put them in the folder system from Lesson 4: an Assets folder for files used in every episode, and an episode folder with subfolders for raw audio, project files, and exports.

In GarageBand, create an Empty Project and choose an Audio track. Create one track for each piece of audio, for example Host, Guest, Intro, and Outro, and name each track so you never confuse them. The tutorial also suggests switching the ruler at the top of the window from bars and beats to time, so you can see exactly how many minutes and seconds your episode lasts.

Then drag each audio file from Finder into its own track. Having each voice on its own track is what makes editing flexible: you can raise a quiet guest without making the host louder, or remove a cough from one person without touching the other.

Arranging the Episode

A typical episode follows a simple order: intro, main episode, outro. In GarageBand, each piece of audio on a track is called a region. Arranging means sliding regions along the timeline so each part starts at the right moment.

The tutorial shows the key move: hold Shift, click to select both voice regions together, and drag them as a pair so the conversation starts where the intro ends. Moving the voice tracks together keeps them in sync, meaning lined up in time. If you moved only one voice, the host would answer questions the guest had not asked yet.

Next, zoom out to see the whole episode, using the zoom slider in the upper right, and drag the outro region to the end of the conversation. Zooming in and out often is a good habit: zoom out to see the shape of the episode, and zoom in to make precise cuts.

Many podcasts let the intro music overlap the first words slightly, so the music fades out as the host begins speaking. Podcasting Pro Basics warns that overlapping regions can add up to a sudden jump in volume and cause clipping, so any overlap should use a fade, which you will set up in Stop 5.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

GarageBand can "snap" regions to the beat grid. For speech, turning snapping off can make small timing moves easier.
Some editors drop markers at each topic change so they can jump around a long recording quickly.

Connection: Film editing

Video editors arrange clips on a timeline exactly like this, keeping the picture and the sound in sync. Move one without the other and the actors' lips no longer match their words.

Cutting and Trimming

The simplest edit is trimming: removing the dead air and small talk before the show really starts and after it ends. Podcasting Pro Basics notes you can usually see these parts in the waveform without even listening, because the shape of the sound changes when the conversation begins.

To remove a section from the middle, GarageBand uses splitting: cutting a region into two pieces at the playhead, the vertical line that shows the current position. The tutorial's method: select all the voice tracks, move the playhead to the start of the part you want to remove, and press Command (⌘) + T to split. Move to the end of that part and split again. Now select only the middle piece and press Delete.

Deleting leaves a gap. Select everything after the gap on both voice tracks and drag it back to close the gap. The tutorial stresses always cutting on every voice track at once: if you cut only one track and moved it, the conversation would fall out of sync. Because cuts shorten the episode, zoom out at the end and move the outro back to where the conversation now ends.

Text-based editors offer another way to cut. Apps such as Riverside create a transcript, a written version of every word, and let you delete words or sentences from the text; the audio is cut to match, much like editing a document. It is fast, but always listen to the result, because a cut that looks fine on paper can sound abrupt.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Command (⌘) + Z undoes your last edit. Save a copy of the project before big edits, so you can always go back.
Cuts sound most natural just before a breath or at the start of a new sentence, where a natural pause already exists.

    Cleaning Up Speech, Honestly

    Spoken recordings are full of small distractions: filler words such as "um," "uh," and "like"; long pauses; false starts; and repeated sentences. Removing some of them makes a show tighter. Removing all of them can make speakers sound robotic, so keep enough natural rhythm that people still sound like themselves. Riverside's tools, for example, can cut some fillers and simply silence others to avoid a choppy sound.

    Breaths are natural; a show with no breaths sounds strange. Rather than deleting every breath, lower the loud ones. When you remove a long pause, leave a short natural gap. If a cut leaves dead digital silence, fill it with a few seconds of the room tone you recorded in Lesson 4, so the background sound stays consistent.

    Listen closely at every cut point. A cut in the middle of a sound wave can create a tiny click. A very short crossfade, where the end of one piece fades out while the next fades in, smooths the join. Also listen for cuts that make a sentence's pitch jump unnaturally, a sign the edit came in the wrong spot.

    Editing has ethical limits. The CRISJ toolkit you shared commits its podcasters to the Society of Professional Journalists Code of Ethics. A key idea from journalism: never edit someone's words to change what they meant. Do not move an answer so it seems to respond to a different question, and do not cut a "not" from a sentence. Trimming for time is fine; changing meaning is not. When in doubt, ask the speaker or keep the original.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Removing long pauses and repeats respects your listeners' time, one of the CRISJ toolkit's core reminders.
    The CRISJ toolkit even warns: don't smack your lips! Mouth clicks are distracting and tedious to remove.

    Connection: Journalism

    Radio and newspaper journalists follow the same rule as podcast editors: quotations can be shortened, but never changed to say something the speaker did not mean.

    Balancing Levels and Fades

    Next, make sure every voice is about equally loud, so listeners do not hear one booming host and one faint guest. Play the episode and watch each track's level meter. The tutorial's guideline: keep it in the green, and yellow at most. If it reaches red, the audio is distorting. Adjust each track's volume slider until the voices sound balanced.

    For changes over time, use volume automation: a line that changes a track's volume at specific moments. In GarageBand, choose Mix, then Show Automation, and set the track's menu to Volume. A yellow line appears. Double-click on the line to add a keyframe, a point you can drag up or down. Two keyframes, one high and one low, create a smooth fade.

    The tutorial uses this to fade the intro music: add a keyframe where the fade should start and another where the intro ends, then drag the second one to the bottom. The music now fades out smoothly as the conversation begins. The same tool lets you lower background music under speech and raise it again between segments, a technique called a music bed when music plays quietly under a voice.

    Effects from Lesson 7 belong here too. Select a track, open Smart Controls, and adjust its compressor and EQ: gentle compression evens out each voice, and a high-pass filter removes rumble. Use the same settings on similar voices so they sound like they were recorded in the same place.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Lowering music automatically whenever someone speaks is called "ducking." Many podcast apps can do it for you.
    Background music should sit well below the voice. If you have to strain to hear a word, the music is too loud.

    Connection: Theatre sound operation

    Theatre sound operators fade music down under an actor's first line and back up at scene changes, using the same idea as volume automation, but live.

    Noise Reduction and Limiting

    Podcasting Pro Basics describes two polishing steps. The first is noise reduction, which lowers steady background hiss and hum. The author's main rule: use it conservatively. Too much noise reduction makes voices sound thin, "tinny," and watery. The goal is to brush away a little hiss, not to change the voice.

    Fact check

    The book says to keep noise reduction "less than 15%." Its own screenshot shows the setting is measured in decibels: the "Light Noise Reduction" preset reduces noise by 15 dB. Many editors start even lower, around 6 to 12 dB, and listen carefully.

    The second step is limiting, which you met in Lesson 7. A limiter raises the overall level while never letting peaks go past a ceiling. The book suggests setting the ceiling extremely close to the maximum. Many podcast editors leave a little more room, setting the ceiling around −1 dB, so the file does not distort after it is converted to MP3.

    Platforms also care about overall loudness, the average level listeners perceive. It is measured in units called LUFS (loudness units relative to full scale). Apple recommends that podcasts aim for about −16 LUFS. Some editing apps, including Audacity, can measure loudness and adjust a file to a target. Matching a common target means your show will not sound much quieter or louder than the episode a listener played before it.

    Order matters: clean up first (noise reduction, EQ), then even out (compression), and limit last. And always compare before and after with headphones: if the "polished" version sounds worse, undo it.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Many noise reduction tools ask you to select a few seconds of pure background noise first, so they know what to remove. Your room tone is perfect for this.
    Reducing noise is easier when the mic is close and the voice is strong compared with the background.

    Exporting the Finished Episode

    When you are happy with the edit, listen to the whole episode one last time from start to finish, without stopping. Then export it. In GarageBand, choose Share, then Export Song to Disk. Give the file a clear name and save it to the episode's Exports folder. While GarageBand writes the file, it shows the word bouncing, an old studio term for combining tracks into one file.

    Choose the format. The tutorial recommends MP3 for spoken podcasts, because the quality difference from WAV is very hard to hear for speech, while WAV files are much larger. Podcasting Pro Basics suggests exporting a speech-only show in mono at 128 kilobits per second, since voices sit in the center and do not need stereo. If your show includes stereo music, stereo export at a slightly higher rate keeps the music sounding full.

    Keep your project file and the full-quality audio, not just the MP3. If you ever need to fix a mistake, you will edit the project and export again, rather than editing the MP3, which would lose quality each time it is saved.

    Finally, check the exported file on a different device, such as a phone with earbuds. Listen for sudden volume jumps, clicks at edit points, music that drowns out words, and the very beginning and end, where accidental cuts often hide. Then the episode is ready to upload to your podcast host or share with your teacher.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Before uploading, add the title, episode number, and your name to the file's information so it is easy to identify.
    Riverside can export video up to 4K for YouTube, and audio-only MP3 for a podcast host.

    Connection: Music production

    Musicians bounce mixes too. A song is exported as a high-quality WAV for mastering and streaming, and smaller files are made only for sharing previews.
