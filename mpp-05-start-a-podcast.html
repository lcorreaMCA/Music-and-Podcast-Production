Stop 1 of 8

Why Most Podcasts Stop

A podcast is a series of audio (and sometimes video) episodes that listeners can stream or download whenever they want, usually by following, or subscribing to, the show in an app. Unlike radio, a podcast is on demand: the listener chooses when to press play.

Starting a podcast is easy; keeping it going is hard. The Riverside "How to Start a Podcast" video you will watch in this lesson says Apple Podcasts lists more than 4 million shows, but only about 500,000 are still active. It adds a commonly cited estimate that most podcasts never make it past their third episode. When a show quietly stops publishing, people call it podfade.

Those numbers come from a company that sells podcast software, so treat them as rough estimates rather than exact facts. The lesson behind them is still true: a show that publishes steadily for 10 to 20 episodes is already ahead of most podcasts. The video's host says he has kept a weekly technology show running for more than 15 years, and he credits planning and passion, not expensive gear.

This lesson walks through the decisions that help a show last: choosing a topic you care about, picking a format and schedule you can keep, setting up simple gear, recording in a good space, and getting your episodes onto the apps where listeners find them.

Choosing Your Niche

A niche is the specific topic or focus of your show. The Riverside host's first piece of advice is to avoid chasing whatever is trending. Instead, picture a Venn diagram with two circles: one for what you are passionate about and one for what you have experience or expertise in. Your best topic sits where the circles overlap, because that is the only kind of topic you will still want to talk about at episode 50.

Expertise does not mean being a professional. A student who has played cello for eight years, cooked family recipes since childhood, or followed a sports team for a decade has real knowledge to share. The New York Times student podcast webinar adds that students are the experts on their own lives, and that some of the best interview guests are parents, grandparents, and neighbors.

Your show also needs a point. New York Times audio producer Phoebe Lett explains that shows promising to talk about "everything" are too broad for new listeners. When the introduction tells listeners what they will hear and why, they can decide the show is worth their time. A clear point might be "we review a new video game every week" or "we interview a different New Orleans musician each episode."

Finally, think about your audience, the specific people you hope will listen. A show for fellow strings students can use musical terms freely; a show for families new to orchestra needs to explain them. Knowing your audience shapes every later decision, from vocabulary to episode length.

A narrow topic, such as "graffiti art in our city," often wins over a huge topic, because the host can go deep and sound genuinely curious.

Format and Category

A podcast's format is the structure each episode follows. Common formats include the solo show (one host talking or telling a story), the co-hosted conversation (two or more regular hosts with good chemistry), the interview (a host asking a guest questions), narrative or documentary storytelling (a scripted story built from narration, interviews, music, and sound), and audio fiction or podcast theater (acted stories with sound effects).

Format and topic often grow together. The New York Times webinar notes that when students hear examples of each format, they discover new ideas: a student who realizes she has great banter with her grandmother might create a conversation show about their family's favorite recipes.

Each format asks for different skills. Solo shows need strong writing and a confident voice. Conversations need chemistry and a plan so they do not ramble. Interviews need research and good follow-up questions. Narrative shows need the most editing, because they combine many recordings into one story.

When you publish, podcast apps ask you to choose a category, such as Music, Education, Comedy, Arts, or News. Podcasting Pro Basics advises choosing one category and sticking with it at first. A consistent category helps the right listeners find you, helps your show appear in rankings, and keeps your episodes consistent.

    Length and Schedule

    Decide on an episode length before you record. Podcasting Pro Basics says there is no single right length, from 15 minutes to more than three hours, but each episode should be about the same length so listeners can predict the time commitment. Student contests set strict limits: the New York Times contest, for example, only accepts podcasts of five minutes or less.

    Next, choose a release schedule: the day and time new episodes appear. The Riverside host notes that the biggest and fastest-growing shows usually publish once a week. If weekly would burn you out, choose every other week or once a month instead. The best schedule is the one you can actually keep.

    Consistency matters for two reasons. Listeners build habits: if a new episode always arrives Monday morning, they look forward to it. And podcast apps notice when many people listen in a short time, which can help a show climb in the charts. A missed week here and there is fine; disappearing for months is how podfade begins.

    A smart trick is to build a small buffer of finished episodes before launch, such as three episodes recorded and edited before the first one goes live. If you get sick or have a busy week of exams, a finished episode is already waiting.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Many podcasters release two or three episodes on launch day so new listeners can sample the show.
    Some shows publish in seasons, such as eight episodes followed by a break. It gives the host time to plan the next set.

    Connection: Music performance

    Orchestras announce a season of concerts months ahead, and audiences plan around it. A steady podcast schedule builds the same trust between a show and its audience.

    Microphones and Headphones

    You learned in Lessons 1 and 2 how microphones work. For a podcast, the Riverside host says an inexpensive USB microphone is the biggest single upgrade over a laptop's built-in mic. His budget pick is the Samson Q2U, a dynamic mic with both USB and XLR outputs; his upgrade pick is the Shure MV7+, which can also connect either way. Both are cardioid dynamic mics that focus on the voice in front of them.

    Fact check

    The video transcript calls the budget mic the "Samsung Q2U." The correct name is the Samson Q2U; Samson is an audio company that has no connection to Samsung.

    Podcasting Pro Basics suggests matching gear to your real plans instead of buying the most expensive setup. Its "Good" setup is one quality USB mic and free software, ideal for a solo show or one guest. Its "Better" setup uses several dynamic mics on stands plugged into a USB mixer, so in-person guests each get their own mic. The author compares buying top-of-the-line gear too early to buying an expensive guitar before you can play a single chord.

    Wear headphones while recording. The Riverside host gives two reasons. First, you hear your own voice through the mic, so you can catch problems such as popping or rustling right away. Second, when you talk to a remote guest, their voice comes through your headphones instead of your speakers. If it came through speakers, your mic would pick it up and create an echo on your track. Wired headphones avoid the small delays that wireless ones can add.

    With a USB mic that has a headphone jack, plug your headphones straight into the mic. This lets you hear yourself with no delay, an example of the direct monitoring you met in Lesson 4.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Giving every speaker their own mic, on their own track, makes editing much easier: you can turn one voice up without changing the others.
    The New York Times webinar points out that a phone's voice memo app is enough to start. Better gear can come later.

    Where and How to Record

    Where you record matters as much as what you record with. Hard, empty rooms with bare walls create echo, also called reverberation. The Riverside host notes that a bedroom often sounds best, because beds, carpets, curtains, and clothes absorb sound. Add pillows or blankets around your space, choose a room with a rug, and turn off fans and noisy appliances. A closet full of clothes can make an excellent small recording booth.

    You can record in person or remotely. In person, hosts can see each other and play off each other's energy, but each mic also picks up the other voices, called bleed, which makes editing harder. It can also be hard to get everyone in the same place at the same time.

    Remote recording lets guests join from anywhere. Apps such as Riverside record each person's audio on their own device and upload it afterward, so a shaky internet connection does not ruin the sound. Each speaker ends up on a separate, clean track. The webinar's student podcaster Daniel suggests a simpler method: call your guest, and ask them to record their own side on their phone and send you the file.

    Before every recording, outline the episode. Even after 15 years, the Riverside host still writes an outline of topics and questions for every show, because it prevents rambling and makes editing faster. You will learn to write full outlines and scripts in the next lesson.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Daniel, a New York Times contest winner, records inside a "pillow fort" he builds around his desk to stop his voice from echoing off hard walls.
    Some apps can reduce echo after recording, but the textbook rule from Lesson 3 still applies: capturing a clean sound is always better than fixing it later.

    Connection: Architecture and design

    Soft materials absorb sound and hard ones reflect it. Interior designers and theatre architects use the same science when they choose carpets, curtains, and wall panels for a space.

    Artwork, Hosting, and Publishing

    To appear in apps such as Apple Podcasts and Spotify, your audio files need a podcast host: a service that stores your episodes and creates your show's RSS feed, the web address that automatically delivers each new episode to listening apps. You submit the RSS feed to each app once, and after that new episodes appear on their own. Podcasting Pro Basics warns that switching hosts later can be difficult, so choose carefully.

    Each show needs cover art, the square image listeners see in their apps. Apple Podcasts requires a square image of at least 1400 by 1400 pixels and at most 3000 by 3000 pixels. Because people often see it as a tiny thumbnail on a phone, the best cover art uses a few bold words and simple, eye-catching shapes. The cover is the "book cover" of your show, so do not skip it.

    Write a strong title and description for every episode. Most shows number their episodes and add a short, catchy title that makes the topic clear, such as "Ep. 4: Why Cellos Sound Like Voices." The description gives a quick summary, so listeners know what they will learn before they press play.

    Finally, share your work. The Riverside host says video has helped his show grow the most: he posts full episodes on YouTube and cuts short vertical clips for social media. For a student project, sharing may be as simple as a SoundCloud link or a class website. Always follow your school's rules about publishing students' voices online.

    Fact check

    Podcasting Pro Basics (2016) recommends submitting your show to "iTunes and Stitcher." Podcasts moved from iTunes to the Apple Podcasts app, and Stitcher shut down in 2023. Today the main destinations include Apple Podcasts, Spotify, and YouTube.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    RSS stands for "Really Simple Syndication." The same technology delivers blog posts and news updates.
    You do not need a new image for every episode; apps show your main cover art by default.
