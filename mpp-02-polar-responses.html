Reading a Polar Pattern

Every microphone listens more in some directions than others. This is its directional response. The direction the mic points, straight at the sound source, is called on-axis, or 0 degrees. Anything arriving from the side or back is off-axis.

Engineers draw a mic's directional response on a circular chart called a polar pattern (also called a pickup pattern). Imagine looking down at the mic from above, with the mic in the center pointing to the top of the page, toward 0°. The rings mark decreasing loudness, usually in steps of a few decibels. The dark outline shows how strongly the mic picks up sound from each angle: where the outline reaches the outer ring, the mic is fully sensitive; where it pulls in toward the center, the mic hears less.

A spot where the mic hears almost nothing is called a null. Nulls are useful: if you aim a null at a noisy fan or a loud drummer, the mic will reject much of that sound. Every pattern in this lesson is defined by where its strong areas and nulls are.

Two physical designs create all the patterns. A pressure mic has a diaphragm open only on the front, sealed at the back, so it simply responds to air pressure from any direction. A pressure-gradient mic has a diaphragm open on both sides, so it responds to the difference in pressure between front and back. As you will see, mixing these two designs makes the heart-shaped patterns found on most microphones.

One more detail: a polar pattern is not exactly the same at every pitch. Most mics become more directional at high frequencies, which is why sound from the sides can come out duller than sound from the front. Good spec sheets show several outlines, one for each frequency.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Polar charts are flat, but a mic listens in 3D. Picture the shape spinning around the mic's axis like a balloon.
On many charts each inner ring marks about 5 dB less pickup; a drop of 6 dB means roughly half the signal strength.

Connection: Mathematics and visual art

Polar graphs use angles and distance from a center point, the same coordinate system used in some mathematics classes. Artists use radial designs, such as rose windows and mandalas, built on the same idea of patterns arranged around a center.

Omnidirectional: Hearing Everything

An omnidirectional mic (often just called an omni) picks up sound equally from all directions. Its polar pattern is a circle. Omni mics are pressure mics: the back of the diaphragm is sealed, so the diaphragm reacts the same way to pressure no matter where the sound comes from.

Hearing everything has advantages. Omni mics capture a natural, open sound that includes the room's ambience, the sound of the space. They have an even tone all the way around, they are less sensitive to wind, handling noise, and popping "p" sounds than directional mics, and they show no proximity effect (the bass boost you will learn about at Stop 5). Classical engineers often reach for omnis first when the hall sounds beautiful.

Hearing everything has a cost too. In a noisy or echoey room, an omni records the noise and echoes along with the performer. If several instruments play in the same room, each omni mic will also pick up its neighbors. So omni mics work best in good-sounding rooms, for groups gathered around one mic, and for tiny clip-on mics that must sound the same as the speaker turns their head.

The Podcasting Pro Basics guide suggests placing one omni mic in the middle of a table when you do not have a mic for every guest. That works, but expect a less upfront, roomier sound than giving each person their own directional mic.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Even an "omni" becomes slightly directional at the highest frequencies, because the mic body itself blocks tiny high-pitched waves.
Most clip-on lavalier mics are omnis, so the voice stays even when the speaker turns or looks down at notes.

Connection: Theatre

Actors in many musicals wear tiny omni mics taped near the hairline. Because the mic hears every direction equally, the voice sounds the same whether the actor faces the audience or turns upstage.

Figure-8: Front and Back

A bidirectional mic picks up sound equally from the front and the back but almost nothing from the sides. Its pattern looks like the number 8, so it is usually called a figure-8. It is the purest pressure-gradient design: both sides of the diaphragm are open to the air, which is why most classic ribbon mics are figure-8s.

Why are the sides silent? A sound arriving from exactly 90° pushes on the front and back of the diaphragm with equal force at the same moment, so the diaphragm does not move and no signal is made. Those side nulls are very deep, which makes figure-8 mics excellent at rejecting a sound that sits off to one side, such as a drum kit beside a singer.

There is a twist. Sound from the back pushes the diaphragm in the opposite direction from sound at the front, so the rear signal is opposite in phase, or "flipped" upside down compared to the front. If you mix the front and back pickup of two different mics carelessly, parts of the sound can cancel. Engineers use this on purpose in special stereo setups at Stop 8.

Figure-8 mics have practical uses. Two podcast hosts can sit face to face with one figure-8 mic between them, each speaking into one side, as Podcasting Pro Basics suggests. A string player can be recorded with the null aimed at a loud neighbor. And in a nice-sounding room, the rear side adds natural room sound.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Old broadcast ribbon mics were figure-8s, so radio actors could stand on both sides of one microphone.
Blow at a sheet of paper held flat in front of you and it moves. Turn it edge-on and blow, and it barely moves. That is the side null.

Connection: Dance and choreography

A figure-8 pattern is a two-person duet: both partners face the mic, and anyone who steps to the side drops out of the picture, like a dancer leaving the light.

The Cardioid Family

Mix an omni (pressure) and a figure-8 (pressure-gradient) response and you get a heart-shaped pattern called a cardioid, from the Greek word for heart. A cardioid hears best from the front, about half as strongly from the sides (roughly 6 decibels quieter), and almost nothing from directly behind, where it has its null at 180°. It is the most common pattern in the world: the SM58, SM7B, and most podcast and stage mics are cardioids.

How does a mic become a cardioid? Many dynamic mics have small openings, called ports, on the sides or back, leading to a winding path inside the mic. Sound from behind reaches both the back and the front of the diaphragm at nearly the same time and with opposite effect, so it cancels. Sound from the front arrives at full strength. The difference in level between front pickup and rear pickup is called front-to-back discrimination.

Tighter versions exist. A supercardioid and a hypercardioid are narrower in front and reject more from the sides, but each has a small pickup lobe at the back, so their nulls point to the rear diagonals instead of straight back. On stage, engineers aim a supercardioid's nulls toward the floor monitors to prevent feedback, the loud squeal that happens when a speaker's sound loops back into a mic.

Many large condenser mics have a switch that changes the pattern. These multi-pattern mics have two diaphragms back to back; mixing their signals in different ways creates omni, cardioid, figure-8, and patterns in between. The Blue Yeti from the last lesson works this way, using three capsules.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Singers who cup the mic's grille block the rear ports, turning a cardioid into something closer to an omni, which can cause feedback.
A small icon on a multi-pattern mic shows which pattern is selected: a circle, a heart, or an 8.

    Proximity Effect and Popping

    Move close to a directional mic and your voice suddenly sounds deeper and bigger. This bass boost is called the proximity effect. It happens with pressure-gradient designs, so cardioids, supercardioids, and especially figure-8 mics show it, usually once the source is closer than about one foot. The closer you get, the stronger the boost. Omni mics, which respond to pressure only, do not show it.

    Proximity effect can be a tool or a problem. Radio announcers and podcasters often work close to the mic on purpose for a warm, "larger-than-life" voice. But too much makes speech muddy and boomy. Engineers control it in three ways: move back from the mic, switch on the mic's low-cut filter (also called a high-pass filter), or use an omni mic for close work.

    Getting close also causes plosives: blasts of air from "p" and "b" sounds that hit the diaphragm and make a thump or pop in the recording. A pop filter, a mesh screen placed between the mouth and the mic, breaks up those blasts. A foam windscreen helps too. Another simple trick from Podcasting Pro Basics is to angle the mic slightly so the stream of air passes by instead of straight in.

    Two more voice problems are worth knowing. Sibilance is harsh, hissing "s," "sh," and "f" sounds; it is reduced with a tool called a de-esser or by aiming the mic slightly off the mouth. And many singers and speakers swing from a whisper to a shout, so engineers ask them to lean back from the mic on loud lines. A steady distance of about six inches to a foot is a good starting point for most podcast voices.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Voice actors measure mic distance with an outstretched hand: the space from thumb to pinky is about six to eight inches.
    Some engineers tape a pencil across the front of a vocal mic to split the stream of air from plosives.

    Connection: Film acting

    Film actors learn to change their voice for the camera: a whisper close to a hidden mic can sound intimate, while a shout needs distance. Voice acting for animation uses the proximity effect to make characters sound huge or gentle.

    Distance: Close, Distant, Accent, Ambient

    Where you put the mic matters as much as which mic you choose. The textbook names four placement styles based on working distance, the space between the mic and the source. Close miking places the mic about 1 inch to 3 feet away. It creates a tight, present sound and mostly shuts out the room, because sound gets much weaker with distance: each time you double the distance, the level drops by about 6 decibels.

    Distant miking places one or more mics 3 feet or farther away. It captures the whole instrument or ensemble in natural balance, plus the sound of the room. A good starting distance is roughly equal to the size of the instrument. This is how orchestras and choirs are often recorded, but it only works if the room sounds good; a bad room adds muddy echoes.

    An accent mic sits at a medium distance from a soloist or section inside a larger group, adding just enough presence without sounding separate from the main pickup. An ambient mic is placed so far away that the room's reverberation is as loud as or louder than the direct sound; engineers mix it in to add space or to capture an audience's applause.

    For string players: the textbook suggests a solo violin or viola mic within 45° of the instrument's front, about 3 to 8 feet away in a hall or 2 to 3 feet in a studio. Closer placement sounds scratchier and more nasal; farther away sounds mellow and round. For cello, aim a mic with a flat response toward the f-holes from 6 inches to 3 feet away. For double bass, aim at the f-holes from 6 inches to 1.5 feet.

    Fact check

    The textbook lists the cello's lowest note, C2, as 56 Hz. C2 is actually about 65 Hz. (Its other numbers for violin, viola, and double bass are correct.)

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    The 6 dB drop for each doubling of distance comes from the inverse square law: sound spreads out over a larger and larger area as it travels.
    A distant mic at the wrong height can sound hollow, because floor reflections arrive slightly late and cancel some frequencies.

    Leakage and the 3:1 Rule

    When an instrument's mic also picks up a nearby instrument, that unwanted sound is called leakage, or bleed. Leakage makes it hard to control each instrument separately in the mix, because turning up the guitar mic also turns up the drums it overheard. Two mics hearing the same sound at slightly different times can also partly cancel each other, which makes the tone thin or hollow.

    The textbook lists ways to reduce leakage: move each mic closer to its own instrument; use directional mics with their nulls toward other players; place movable sound-absorbing walls, called gobos or baffles, between instruments; spread the musicians farther apart; put loud instruments or vocals in a separate isolation booth; or plug electric instruments straight into the recording system through a direct box instead of miking an amp.

    When several mics are close to their sources, engineers follow the 3:1 rule: for every unit of distance between a mic and its source, the next mic should be at least three times that distance away. If your violin mic is 1 foot from the violin, the cello mic should be at least 3 feet from the violin mic. Some engineers go further and use a 5:1 rule.

    Leakage is not always bad. A little bleed between mics can add depth and "glue" a live band together, which is why some of the most exciting records were made with everyone playing in one room. The goal is to control leakage, not to fear it.

    Louisiana connection: Cosimo Matassa's small room

    In the late 1940s and 1950s, engineer Cosimo Matassa recorded Fats Domino, Little Richard, and many other stars in his J&M studio on North Rampart Street in New Orleans, a room only about 15 by 16 feet. With the whole band in that small space, leakage was unavoidable. Drummer Earl Palmer later said Matassa could get, with three mics, a sound other engineers needed two dozen mics to match, because he knew exactly where to place each one. That "big beat" sound helped launch rock and roll.

    Stereo Pairs

    Two ears let you hear where sounds come from. A stereo recording uses two channels, left and right, to recreate that sense of space. The English engineer Alan Blumlein patented stereo recording in 1931 and filmed demonstrations of people walking and talking across a room so listeners could hear the voices move. Stereo mic techniques use two or more mics placed in a pattern, and the four basic ones are below.

    A spaced pair (also called A/B) uses two matching mics placed a few feet to many feet apart in front of the source. Sound reaches the nearer mic first, so the pair captures differences in both timing and loudness. The result is wide and spacious, but when the two channels are combined into one (mono), the timing differences can cancel parts of the sound.

    An X/Y pair places two cardioid mics with their capsules as close together as possible, angled 90° to 135° apart. Because the capsules are in the same spot, sound arrives at both at the same time, and only the loudness differs. X/Y gives a solid, focused image with no phase problems when played in mono. A Blumlein pair is the same idea using two figure-8 mics crossed at 90°, which adds more room sound. A popular in-between setup, ORTF, places two cardioids about 17 centimeters apart (about the distance between your ears) and angled 110°; it was developed by the French national broadcaster ORTF.

    The M/S technique (mid-side) pairs a cardioid pointing at the source, the "mid," with a figure-8 turned sideways, the "side." A decoder or plug-in combines them: mid plus side becomes the left channel, and mid minus side becomes the right. Its superpower is that you can change the stereo width later, while mixing, and in mono the side signals cancel out completely, leaving only the clear mid mic.

    The Decca tree, created by engineers at Decca Records in London in the 1950s, uses three omni mics in a triangle hung high above the conductor, with the center mic slightly forward. The spacing varies with the room and orchestra, from about 2 to more than 6 feet between the left and right mics. It is still a standard for orchestral recordings and film scores.

    Specialty Mics and Connections

    Some jobs need special shapes. A shotgun microphone is a long tube with slots along its sides attached to a cardioid capsule. Sound from the sides enters the slots and partly cancels, while sound from the front travels straight down the tube, making it extremely directional. Film crews hang shotguns on a long pole called a boom just above the camera frame to catch actors' dialogue.

    A lavalier, or "lav," is a tiny clip-on mic, usually an omni electret, hidden on clothing for TV, theatre, film, and video interviews. Its biggest enemy is rubbing: a jacket brushing against it creates loud handling noise, so placement is everything. A boundary microphone (also called a PZM, for Pressure Zone Microphone) lies flat on a table, floor, or wall. Sitting right on the surface, it avoids the hollow sound that floor reflections can cause, which makes it handy for meetings, stage floors, and inside pianos.

    Louisiana connection: Hollywood South

    Louisiana is one of the busiest film and television production states in the country, thanks to tax incentives that brought many productions to New Orleans, Baton Rouge, and Shreveport. On those sets, sound crews use exactly the tools in this stop: shotguns on booms above the frame and lavalier mics hidden under costumes.
