Stop 1 of 9

Why Review and Revise

Revision means re-seeing your work and making it better, not just fixing typos. At NPR, every story passes through an editor before it airs, and NPR's judges specifically reward students who spent time editing: cutting unnecessary information and repetition so the main ideas come through clearly.

The Berklee mixing course makes a similar point about mixing: listen, adjust what does not sound right, then listen again, slowly bringing the whole picture into focus, the way a videographer adjusts a camera lens. Each listen-and-adjust cycle is called an iteration.

In this lesson you will review in three passes. First, a self-review of your rough cut using the project rubric. Second, a peer review, where classmates listen and give feedback. Third, a technical review with a checklist. Then you will revise, export your final mix, and reflect.

Remember the Work vs. Play balance from Lesson 4. Review is not about making the episode perfect forever; many records never come out because artists keep changing them. The goal is to make the most important improvements before the deadline, then say "It's done!"

Listening Like a Producer

The Berklee "Listening Like a Producer" lesson lists skills that help producers judge a recording: responding to its emotional content without unwanted prejudice; reacting as though it is the first time you are hearing it, even on the hundredth listen; listening on many levels at once; and noting possible problems without letting them pull you out of the emotion of the record.

It also points out that everyone has prejudices about music and sound, preferences and dislikes that can get in the way of hearing a piece fairly. A reviewer who hates country music, or who finds sports boring, may judge a podcast unfairly. Good reviewers notice their prejudices and set them aside to hear what the creator was trying to do.

Use Berklee's routine for every review. Choose a quiet place, ideally with good headphones, and silence your phone. Let go of prejudices and stay open to the piece. Keep a blank page or document ready to write short, quick notes while listening. Immediately after it ends, expand your notes while your reaction is fresh.

Berklee also suggests naming exact emotions and physical responses. Instead of "it was good," write "I felt curious at the hook, and nostalgic during the grandfather's story; I leaned in when the trumpet came in." Specific reactions are far more useful to a creator than general praise.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Berklee lists physical responses such as goosebumps, foot tapping, holding your breath, or smiling. These are honest signs of how a piece is working.
Listening on many levels means hearing the story, the voices, the music, and the technical quality all at once.

Stop 3 of 9

Self-Review With the Rubric

Begin with your own self-assessment: scoring your rough cut honestly with the project rubric from Lesson 12. For each of the five criteria (content and point; structure and flow; voices and sound; editing, music, and mix; process and professionalism), give yourself a score out of 20 and write one sentence of evidence.

Evidence means something specific you can point to: "My point is stated at 0:24, which is too late; I will move it before 0:15," not "my point is good." A timestamp, the exact minute and second where something happens, makes evidence precise and easy to find when editing.

Then choose your top three improvements. Professional editors know that not every problem is equally important. Fix the problems that most affect the listener first: a confusing beginning, a missing point, or a word covered by music matters more than a tiny click at 3:42.

Finally, write one question you want your peer reviewers to answer, such as "Is it clear who my guest is?" or "Does the music fit the mood?" Asking a focused question gets you focused, useful feedback.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

An honest self-score helps you; inflating it only hides problems you could still fix.

Stop 4 of 9

Giving Peer Feedback

In peer review, classmates listen to each other's work and give feedback. Good feedback follows three rules: it is kind (about the work, not the person), specific (pointing to exact moments, with timestamps), and helpful (suggesting something the creator can actually do). This matches the studio etiquette from Lesson 4: talk about the sound, not the person.

Use a two-part protocol. Warm feedback names what works and why: "The hook at 0:05 made me curious because I didn't know what the sound was." Cool feedback names what could be stronger, framed as a suggestion or question: "At 2:10 the music covers the guest's words; could the bed be lower?" Give at least two warm and two cool comments.

Answer the creator's focused question first, then use the rubric categories to organize your other comments. Avoid vague comments like "it's good" or "make it better," and avoid rewriting the creator's idea into your own. Your job is to help them make their podcast the best version of itself.

Listen to each episode at least twice: once all the way through for feeling and story, and once with pauses to note timestamps. Write your feedback down so the creator can use it during revision.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Try: "I noticed...", "I wondered...", "What if...?", and "The strongest moment was..."
Give every classmate the same careful listen you hope to receive.

Connection: Theatre and music rehearsal

Directors and conductors give notes after a run-through: specific, actionable, and about the performance. Peer feedback works the same way.

    The Technical Checklist

    Next, check the sound itself. Listen on headphones and then on a phone speaker, and go through this checklist. Levels: voices are balanced, and the meter never reaches red; no part clips or distorts. Noise: no hum, buzz, fan noise, or handling thumps distract from the voices. Plosives and sibilance: no popping "p" sounds or harsh "s" sounds.

    Edits: every cut is smooth, with no clicks, no cut-off words, and no unnatural jumps; gaps are filled with room tone. Music: the theme fits the mood, the bed sits below the voices, and fades are smooth. Space: voices sound like they were recorded in a similar place, without distracting echo.

    Length and format: the export is between 3:00 and 5:00 and saved as an MP3 with a clear name. Loudness: the episode is about as loud as a professional podcast when played back to back, with a limiter preventing peaks above about −1 dB. Beginning and end: nothing is clipped off at the very start or end.

    Check mono too: many people listen on a single phone speaker, so make sure nothing important disappears when left and right are combined. Finally, check the file on a second device. Problems that hide on studio headphones often appear on earbuds.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Fix recording problems first (re-record if needed), then edits, then levels, then effects, then loudness.
    If one narration line has a bad plosive, re-recording that line is often faster than repairing it.

    The Content and Ethics Check

    Now review what the episode says. Hook: does something grab the listener within the first 15 seconds? Point: could a listener say what the episode is about after the intro? Arc: is there a clear beginning, middle, and end, and does each section move the story forward? Clarity: would someone who knows nothing about the topic understand it?

    Repetition: is anything said twice that could be said once? Apply the chopping principle from Lesson 6: cut anything that does not support the point. Ending: does the outro leave the listener with a takeaway, a question, or a feeling, rather than just stopping?

    Accuracy: check every name, date, number, and fact against a reliable source. Professional shows are fact-checked; yours should be too. Honest editing: make sure no guest's words were cut or rearranged in a way that changes their meaning.

    Permissions and rights: confirm that everyone you recorded agreed to be recorded and knows where the episode will be shared, that all music is your own original work, and that any sound effects are your own recordings or properly licensed. For a contest, also check the official rules one more time.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Ask someone outside the class, such as a family member, to listen and tell you the point in one sentence.
    Keep a list of the sources you used to check facts, and add it to your process log.

    Connection: Journalism

    Before a radio story airs, editors check facts, fairness, and permissions. Your content and ethics check is the same step professional newsrooms never skip.

    Revise, Export, and Share

    Gather everything: your self-review, your peer feedback, and your technical and content checklists. Make a revision plan, a short list of the changes you will make, in order of importance. You do not have to take every suggestion; you are the producer. But for each piece of feedback you decide not to use, be able to explain why.

    Revise in a copy of your project, such as "Project_v2," so you can always go back. After each major change, listen again from the beginning: a fix in one place can create a new problem somewhere else. Then export the final mix following Lesson 12's checklist, and listen to the whole file one last time on a different device.

    Submit your deliverables: the final MP3, your organized project folder, your script drafts, and your process log. If you plan to enter a contest, check the current official rules and deadline. For NPR, your teacher must submit the entry; for The New York Times, you submit through the Learning Network's form during the contest window.

    Sharing with a real audience is the point of all this work. Whether your episode goes to a national contest, your class, your family, or a school website, follow your school's rules about publishing students' voices online, and make sure everyone in the episode is comfortable with how it will be shared.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Write a short "changelog" in your process log: what you changed between the rough cut and the final mix, and why.
    Hold a class listening party: play the final episodes and celebrate what each team created.

    Reflection and Portfolio

    Reflection means thinking back on your work to understand what you learned and how you will grow. Write a short reflection of about one paragraph for each question: What are you most proud of in your episode? What was the hardest problem you solved, and how? What did feedback change in your work? What would you do differently next time?

    Many contests and art programs ask for an artist statement: a short explanation, in your own words, of what you made, why, and how. For a podcast, describe your question, your audience, your choices about voices, sound, and music, and what you hope listeners take away. Keep it to about 150 words.

    Add your episode to a portfolio, a collection of your best work that shows your skills over time. Include the final MP3, your artist statement, your best script draft, and a few process log entries that show how the episode grew. A portfolio is useful for college applications, summer programs, and jobs in music, media, and communications.

    Look back at the whole course: microphones and polar patterns, the recording process, scripts, effects, editing, and the contests. You now know how professionals turn an idea into a finished piece of audio. Every future project, a song, a podcast season, or a soundtrack, will follow the same path.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Compare your first recording of the course with your final mix. The difference is your growth, in sound.
    Many podcasters plan a second episode right away while the skills are fresh.
