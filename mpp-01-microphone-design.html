Stop 1 of 8

Sound Becomes Signal

Every song, podcast, and film soundtrack you hear began as vibrating air. A microphone (often shortened to mic) is usually the first device in a recording. Its job is to catch those vibrations and turn them into an electrical copy that a computer can store, edit, and play back.

A device that changes one form of energy into a matching form of a different energy is called a transducer. A mic changes sound waves in the air into electrical signals in a wire. A guitar is a transducer too: it turns string vibrations into sound waves. A speaker works in the opposite direction from a mic, turning electrical signals back into moving air. Even your ear is a transducer, turning sound waves into nerve signals for your brain.

The path a sound travels from the performer to the listener is called the signal chain. A simple chain looks like this: voice, then microphone, then preamp, then audio interface, then computer, then speakers or headphones. A recording can only be as good as the weakest link in that chain, so engineers fix problems at the microphone before they press record instead of hoping to "fix it in the mix."

Engineers sum this up with the Good Rule: a good musician, plus a good instrument, plus a good performance, plus good acoustics (the sound of the room), plus a good mic, plus good placement, equals a good sound. If any one link falls short, the whole track suffers. The textbook adds two more rules worth remembering: there are no strict rules in mic choice, only guidelines, so experiment; and trust your ears.

A record player cartridge, a tape head, and a speaker are all transducers. Each changes energy from one form into another.

Connection: Photography

A camera is a transducer for light, the way a microphone is a transducer for sound. A photographer chooses a lens for the shot, and an engineer chooses a mic for the sound. Both know the picture or the recording is only as good as what reaches the sensor.

The Dynamic Microphone

Inside every microphone is a diaphragm: a thin, flexible surface that moves back and forth when sound waves hit it, a bit like the skin of a drum. The three main kinds of microphones are named after how they turn that motion into electricity: dynamic, ribbon, and condenser.

A dynamic microphone works by electromagnetic induction: whenever a metal wire moves through a magnetic field, a small electric current is created in the wire. In a dynamic mic, a tiny coil of wire called the voice coil is glued to the back of the diaphragm and hangs inside the field of a permanent magnet. When sound pushes the diaphragm, the coil moves with it, and a current that copies the sound wave flows out of the mic. This is why dynamic mics are also called moving-coil mics.

Because the diaphragm carries the weight of the coil, it is heavier than the diaphragms in other mics. A heavier diaphragm reacts a little more slowly, so dynamic mics capture less fine high-frequency detail. In exchange, they are tough. A dynamic mic can survive being dropped, handle very loud sounds without distorting, and does not need any power supply to work.

That toughness explains where you see them. The Shure SM58 is the most common live vocal mic in the world, and its close cousin, the SM57, is a studio favorite for snare drums and guitar amplifiers. Many podcasters also choose dynamic mics, such as the Shure SM7B, because they mostly pick up the voice right in front of them and much less of the room around it. That saves a lot of cleanup time when you are not recording in a soundproof studio.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Dynamic mics like the SM58 are famous for surviving drops, spilled drinks, and years of touring.
The name comes from the moving coil: the part that makes the signal is in motion, which is what "dynamic" means.

The Ribbon Microphone

A ribbon microphone also uses electromagnetic induction, so it is really a special kind of dynamic mic. Instead of a coil, it uses an extremely thin strip of metal, usually aluminum, hung between the poles of a strong magnet. The ribbon itself is the diaphragm. In older designs it is about 2 microns thick, which is far thinner than a human hair. The ribbon is often folded into tiny pleats, called corrugations, to make it flexible and stronger.

Because a ribbon is so light, it reacts quickly and captures a smooth, natural, "warm" sound. Engineers love ribbons on brass, guitar amps, strings, and voices that sound harsh on other mics. However, a single ribbon makes a very small signal, much weaker than a coil with many turns of wire. So most ribbon mics contain a step-up transformer, a part that boosts the ribbon's tiny voltage to a usable level before it leaves the mic.

Ribbon mics have a history. They ruled radio and recording studios in the 1930s and 1940s, then fell out of fashion when smaller, sturdier dynamic and condenser mics arrived. Old ribbons were also fragile: a strong blast of air or a dropped mic could stretch the ribbon and ruin it. Today companies such as Royer, AEA, Beyerdynamic, and Coles build modern ribbons that are much more durable, and some even contain a small amplifier powered the same way condenser mics are.

Here is one safety habit to learn now: always handle ribbon mics gently, keep them away from wind and loud blasts of air, and turn off phantom power (you will learn what it is in the next stop) before plugging or unplugging any microphone. A bad cable with phantom power on can damage an older ribbon.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Classic ribbon mics like the RCA 44 appear in many old radio and newsreel photos, and some broadcasters still use ribbons today.
Many classic ribbons slowly roll off the highest frequencies, which is part of why they sound smooth and less bright.

Connection: Theatre and film costume design

A costume designer picks fabric for how it moves under stage lights. An engineer picks a ribbon mic for how it softens a bright trumpet. In both cases the material itself shapes what the audience experiences.

The Condenser Microphone

A condenser microphone works in a completely different way. Instead of magnets, it uses a capacitor, an electrical part that stores an electric charge between two plates. (In the United Kingdom a capacitor is still called a "condenser," which is where the name comes from.) One plate is a very thin, movable diaphragm, and the other is a fixed backplate. When sound pushes the diaphragm closer to the backplate or lets it move away, the amount of charge the pair can hold changes, and so does the voltage. That changing voltage is the audio signal.

Condenser mics need electricity for two reasons: to charge the plates and to run a tiny amplifier built inside the mic. Most get it through phantom power, a steady +48 volts sent from the mixer, preamp, or audio interface up the same XLR cable that carries the sound. The voltage travels equally on pins 2 and 3, the two wires that carry the audio, and returns through pin 1, the ground. Because the voltage is equal on both audio wires, it stays invisible to the sound. On most interfaces you turn it on with a button labeled 48V.

The condenser diaphragm is extremely light, so it follows the sound wave very accurately, especially the high frequencies. That is why large condenser mics are the usual choice for studio vocals, acoustic guitar, piano, and strings. The trade-offs: condensers are more delicate, more sensitive to humidity and bumps, usually more expensive, and they pick up everything in the room, including the air conditioner and the dog barking next door.

Two variations are worth knowing. An electret condenser has a charge permanently built into its diaphragm or backplate, so it needs only a little power for its internal amplifier, often from a battery or from the device it plugs into. Electrets are cheap and can be tiny: the mics in phones, laptops, and the small clip-on mics on news anchors are usually electrets. A tube condenser uses a vacuum tube as its amplifier and needs its own external power supply box; engineers prize tube mics for their rich, slightly warm color.

Fact check

One textbook diagram describes the ground pin as carrying "−48 volts." That is not quite right: phantom power is +48 volts on pins 2 and 3, measured against pin 1, which is ground (0 volts). Another diagram in the Berklee handbook draws a magnet inside a condenser mic. Condenser capsules do not use magnets; only dynamic and ribbon mics do.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Because the plates carry a charge, condensers attract dust. A pop filter and a cover when the mic is not in use help it last for decades.
Phones and many cameras send a few volts called "plug-in power" to small electret mics. It is not the same as 48-volt phantom power.

    How a Mic Responds

    Two microphones can hear the same singer and still sound different. Engineers describe those differences with a few measurements. The first is frequency response: a graph of how loudly a mic reproduces each pitch, from the lowest bass people can hear (about 20 hertz, or Hz) to the highest treble (about 20,000 Hz, or 20 kilohertz). Hertz means vibrations per second.

    A mic that reproduces every frequency at the same level has a flat frequency response, which gives an accurate, uncolored picture of the source. Other mics are designed with a presence boost, a small rise in the upper middle frequencies that helps a voice cut through a band. Neither is "better." A flat mic suits a classical violin; a mic with a presence boost might suit a rock singer. Remember too that a mic usually sounds different off-axis, meaning from the side or back, so sounds leaking in from other directions can come out dull or uneven.

    At the very bottom of the range is rumble: low vibrations from trucks, air conditioners, footsteps, or a bumped stand that travel through the floor into the mic. Engineers fight rumble with a shock mount (a holder that hangs the mic in elastic bands), with a mic that does not pick up the lowest frequencies, or with a low-cut filter, a switch that turns down the deepest bass.

    Transient response measures how quickly a diaphragm reacts to a sudden sound, such as a drum hit, a plucked string, or the "t" in a word. The beginning of a sound is called its transient. The figure below shows the same woodblock hit recorded by three mics. The dynamic mic, with its heavier diaphragm and coil, reacts most slowly and smooths the attack. The ribbon reacts faster. The condenser, with the lightest diaphragm, tracks the hit most accurately.

    Specs only tell part of the story. Many qualities that make a mic sound "warm," "airy," or "punchy" have no standard measurement. The final test is always your own ears: record the same source with two mics and listen back.

    Explore: Which mic for which job?

    Loud, punchy sources: a dynamic mic handles the volume and softens harsh attacks.

    Detailed, natural tone: a condenser with a flat response, or a ribbon if the instrument sounds too bright.

    A dynamic mic close to the mouth captures the voice and less of the untreated room.

    Condensers placed farther back capture the whole group and the beautiful room sound.

    Connection: Visual art

    A painter chooses between a fine sable brush and a wide flat brush. A detailed condenser and a forgiving dynamic mic work the same way: each tool shapes the texture of the final work.

    Output Specs and Cables

    When you read a mic's specifications, a few numbers describe its output. Sensitivity tells how strong a signal the mic produces for a standard sound level. A more sensitive mic puts out a stronger signal. In general, condensers are the most sensitive because of their built-in amplifier, dynamics come next, and passive ribbons are the least sensitive. A low-sensitivity mic on a quiet source needs a lot of extra boost, and that boost can bring up background hiss.

    Self-noise is the faint hiss a mic makes on its own, even in a silent room. In condensers it comes mostly from the built-in amplifier. For quiet sources recorded from far away, such as a solo harp in a large hall, a mic with low self-noise matters a lot.

    At the other extreme, very loud sounds can overload a mic and cause distortion. Dynamic mics are hard to overload. A condenser capsule can produce so much signal that it overloads its own internal amplifier, so many condensers have a pad switch, often marked −10 dB, that lowers the level right after the capsule. Use the pad for loud sources like a trumpet bell or a kick drum, and switch it off for normal sources, because it also lowers the mic's signal compared to its hiss.

    Impedance is a measure, in ohms, of how much a device resists the flow of an electrical signal. Professional mics are low-impedance (around 150 to 250 ohms), which lets them run on cables hundreds of feet long without losing treble or picking up much noise. High-impedance mics lose high frequencies on long cables, so they are rarely used in professional recording.

    Professional mics also use a balanced line: a cable with two signal wires plus a ground wire. Any hum or buzz that sneaks into the cable lands on both signal wires equally, and the input cancels it out, keeping only the music. These cables end in an XLR connector, the round three-pin plug you see on most microphone cables. The standard wiring is pin 1 ground, pin 2 positive ("hot"), and pin 3 negative ("cold"). An instrument cable for an electric guitar, by contrast, is usually unbalanced, which is why long guitar cables can pick up hum.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    In some big studios, a mic that has been used on a kick drum is labeled and kept for kick drums only, because loud blasts can slowly stretch a diaphragm.
    The two signal wires inside a mic cable are twisted together so that outside noise hits both equally and cancels.

    Connection: Theatre sound design

    In a theatre, cables can run from the stage to a booth at the back of the house. Balanced XLR lines are what let a whispered line of dialogue travel that far and still sound clean.

    Preamps and Choosing Your Mics

    The signal coming out of a microphone is tiny, far too weak for a recorder or computer to use. A microphone preamp (short for preamplifier, and nicknamed a "pre") boosts it, often by 30 to 70 decibels, up to a strong level called line level. The knob that sets that boost is called gain. Every audio interface and mixer with a mic input has preamps built in, and some engineers buy separate preamps for their low noise or their special color.

    Some mics need more gain than others. The Shure SM7B, for example, has low sensitivity, so it needs a preamp with plenty of clean gain; some podcasters add an inline booster, and newer versions such as the SM7dB have a booster built in. A USB microphone takes a shortcut: it contains its own preamp and a converter that turns the signal into digital data, so it plugs straight into a computer, tablet, or phone without an interface.

    How should you build a microphone collection? The textbook describes two paths. One is to buy a small set of versatile mics that cover many jobs, which is ideal for a school or home studio on a budget. The other is to collect many specialized mics, each considered best for one instrument, which suits big professional studios. For a podcast, a good starting kit is one dynamic or condenser mic per voice, headphones, and either USB mics or XLR mics into an interface.

    Fact check

    The Podcasting Pro Basics guide calls the Blue Yeti "both dynamic and condenser." The Yeti is a condenser mic only: it has three condenser capsules that let you switch between pickup patterns. The same guide says condenser mics must plug into a soundboard; USB condensers like the Yeti plug straight into a computer. Also, the podcast video transcript names the "Samsung Q2U"; the mic is the Samson Q2U, a dynamic mic with both USB and XLR outputs.

    Whatever you choose, the textbook's first rule still applies: there are no strict rules, only guidelines. Start with what you have, listen carefully, move the mic, and try again. Engineers learn more from careful experiments than from any spec sheet.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Some famous preamps are valued because they add a pleasant color, often from tubes or transformers, not because they are perfectly clean.
    The same XLR cable carries the tiny mic signal toward the preamp and the 48-volt phantom power back toward the mic at the same time.

    Connections: Mics Across the Arts

    The same microphone ideas show up in every kind of sound work. A music producer, a podcast producer, and a composer writing for film or theatre all start by asking the same question: what do I want the audience to hear, and which tool will capture it best?     Connection: Dance

    Choreographers count on sound reaching dancers on time. In a theatre, the mics that pick up live musicians feed the monitors the dancers hear, so mic choice affects movement on stage too.
