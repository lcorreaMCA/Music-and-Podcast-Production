Stop 1 of 9

The People in the Room

A recording is a team project. The artist is the performer or group whose music or voice is being recorded. Studio musicians are skilled players hired to perform on a recording, and an arranger decides which instruments play which parts.

The producer guides the whole project, a bit like a film director. The producer helps choose the songs or episode topics, coaches the performers, decides when a take is good enough, keeps the session on schedule and budget, and keeps everyone positive. One Berklee producer describes the job as being a coach: set a confident, upbeat mood and keep the session moving forward.

The recording engineer handles the technical side: choosing and placing mics, setting levels, running the recording software, and making sure every sound is captured cleanly. An assistant engineer sets up mics, cables, and headphones, keeps notes, and often arrives early to prepare the room. A maintenance engineer keeps the equipment working.

At the end of the process, a mastering engineer gives the finished mixes their final polish so they sound good together and on every speaker. In a school or home studio, one or two people often wear every one of these hats. On a podcast, the host may also be the producer, the engineer, and the editor.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

In one Berklee example, the assistant engineer arrives one hour early and the recording engineer 30 minutes early, so the room is ready when the singer walks in.
A record producer is closer to a film director than to a film producer: the director guides the performances, as a record producer does.

Connection: Theatre

A stage production has a director, a stage manager, and a crew. A recording session has a producer, an engineer, and assistants. In both, clear roles let the performers focus on their art.

The Signal Chain Journey

In Lesson 1 you learned that a microphone is a transducer. Recording is a whole chain of transducers and devices. The Berklee vocal production course lists the journey in order. During recording and monitoring: (1) the source, such as a singer; (2) the recording environment, the room and its effect on the sound; (3) the microphone; (4) routers, such as the analog-to-digital converter that turns the electrical signal into numbers; and (5) modifiers, such as the preamp, EQ, and compression.

During playback and mixing: (6) storage, the recording software and audio files; (7) the digital-to-analog converter that turns the numbers back into an electrical signal; (8) more modifiers, such as EQ, reverb, and delay; (9) the studio monitor speakers, which are transducers again; (10) the listening room; and finally (11) your ear and brain.

The first two links get special attention. The Berklee course explains that a very echoey room adds a sense of distance that is hard to remove later. A drier room, one with soft, absorbent surfaces, gives you more choices when mixing, because you can always add reverb but you cannot easily take it away. That is why studios use small vocal booths or movable walls called gobos, and why a closet full of clothes can make a surprisingly good podcast booth.

Remember the weakest-link idea: each step can add noise or color. Converting sound to electricity and back can never be perfect, so engineers aim to capture the best possible sound at the start rather than fix it later.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

People love singing in the shower because hard tile adds reverb. For a vocal recording, that sound is an effect, not your everyday setting.
The A/D converter measures the signal tens of thousands of times per second; 44,100 or 48,000 times per second are common rates.

Connection: Architecture

Architects shape how a room sounds with its size, shape, and materials. Concert halls, churches, and studios are designed with acoustics in mind, just like the vocal booth in a recording studio.

Step 1: Preparation

The textbook divides the recording process into eight steps: preparation, recording, overdubbing, mixdown, mastering, song sequence editing, product manufacturing, and marketing and sales. The rest of this lesson walks through them in order.

Preparation happens before anyone presses the red record button, and the textbook calls it one of the most important steps. The team answers questions like these: What is the goal? What is the budget, and how much studio time can we afford? Is the band practiced enough? Who will speak for the group when there is a disagreement? How will the music be distributed, and to whom? Are there legal issues, such as permission to use someone else's song or music?

For a podcast, preparation means choosing a topic you care about and know well, planning the format, and writing an outline. The podcast guides you uploaded agree on this. The Riverside host still outlines every weekly episode after 15 years, and Podcasting Pro Basics suggests about ten topics in a natural order, plus backup questions in case a guest gives very short answers. The New York Times webinar adds that a podcast should have a clear point that listeners can hear in the first minute.

Preparation also includes the room and the gear. Choose the quietest space you can find, turn off notifications, and test every microphone before the guest arrives. The textbook says the vocalist is the most important "device" in the chain, so make sure singers and speakers are comfortable: water, a good temperature, and enough time to warm up.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

The CRISJ podcasting toolkit you shared reminds hosts to plan a narrative arc, a beginning, middle, and end, so listeners understand why the conversation unfolds the way it does.
Using a song you did not write in a podcast or video usually requires permission. Royalty-free music libraries are a safer choice.

Step 2: Recording and Setting Levels

In the recording step, sound sources are captured to separate tracks in a DAW (digital audio workstation), the recording software on the computer, such as GarageBand, Audacity, or Pro Tools. Each track holds one source. This idea is called isolation: with the guitar on one track and the voice on another, you can change the level, position, or tone of one without affecting the other.

Good engineers stay organized. They label every track (Vox, Guitar L, Host 1) and write down which mic and input each source uses on a track sheet. That way, if someone needs to come back next week to fix a line, the same mic can be set up the same way.

Next comes gain staging, setting levels at each step so the signal is strong but never distorted. The textbook's method: set the faders at their unity setting (0 dB, meaning no change), start with the EQ flat, ask each performer to sing or play their loudest part, and turn up the preamp gain while watching the track meter. The goal is a healthy signal that never reaches the red clip light, which means the signal is too loud and is being chopped off, causing harsh distortion that usually cannot be fixed.

Fact check

Podcasting Pro Basics tells you to keep levels "between −0.05 and 1." Digital meters do not work that way. They measure in dBFS (decibels below full scale), where 0 is the absolute maximum. A safe target for most voice and music recording is peaks around −12 to −6 dBFS, which leaves headroom, room for surprise loud moments. The book's main advice is right, though: a recording that is a bit too quiet can be fixed, but one that clips usually cannot.

Before the real take, run through the whole song or episode outline once. Listen for buzzes, hums, squeaky chairs, or a phone vibrating on the table, and fix them now. Many engineers record the practice run too, because it sometimes turns out to be the best take.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Some engineers always plug the kick drum into input 1 and the snare into input 2, so they never have to think about which track is which.
Podcasting Pro Basics says it plainly: the moment the show ends, save your session.

The Headphone Mix

While recording, performers listen through headphones to a separate blend of the tracks made just for them. This is called the cue mix, or headphone mix. It can be different from what the engineer hears in the control room. A drummer may want more bass; a singer may want more of their own voice. Studios with several cue systems can give each musician their own mix.

A good cue mix can make or break a performance. If singers cannot hear themselves, they may push too hard or sing off pitch. The Berklee course gives a smart tip: if a singer asks for more of their voice, it is often better to turn the music down and then raise the whole headphone level, instead of just turning the vocal up.

Volume matters for health and accuracy. The textbook warns that very loud headphones can make pitches seem slightly off, so instruments should not be tuned while wearing headphones. Some singers take one ear cup off so they can hear their natural voice in the room. Keeping headphones at a moderate level also protects your hearing during long sessions.

Podcasters need headphones too. The Riverside video explains that hosts should wear headphones so they hear their own voice through the mic and hear remote guests in their ears, not through computer speakers, where the guest's voice would leak back into the host's microphone and cause echo. Closed-back headphones, which seal around the ear, leak the least sound.

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

Some podcasters wear small in-ear monitors so they barely show on camera when the show is also filmed for YouTube.
Many people dislike hearing their recorded voice at first, because they usually hear it through the bones of their head, which adds bass.

Connection: Theatre and dance

Stage performers use monitor speakers or in-ear monitors for the same reason singers use a cue mix: to hear the band and themselves clearly so they can stay in time and in tune.

Step 3: Overdubbing

Once the basic tracks are recorded, new parts can be added on top. In overdubbing, a performer listens to the earlier tracks in headphones while recording a new part onto another track, such as a harmony vocal, a guitar solo, or a second violin line. Many sessions begin with a scratch vocal, a rough guide vocal recorded with the band so everyone knows where they are in the song, which is replaced later by a carefully overdubbed final vocal.

If a take is almost perfect except for one wrong note or line, you do not need to redo the whole thing. Punching in means re-recording just that small section, switching into record right before the mistake and out right after. Done well, listeners never know the part was recorded in pieces. When overdubbing, try to match the room sound of the original tracks, so the new part does not sound like it was recorded somewhere else.

Guitarist and inventor Les Paul was a pioneer of overdubbing. With singer Mary Ford, he layered many guitar parts and many vocal harmonies, all performed by just the two of them, on hits such as "How High the Moon" (1951), recorded in his home studio. Their records sounded like a whole band and a choir.

Overdubbing has not always been possible. At Cosimo Matassa's J&M studio in New Orleans, early sessions were cut straight onto a disc, so overdubs and corrections were impossible: if anyone made a mistake, the disc was thrown away and the band started over. That is the setting where Fats Domino recorded "The Fat Man" on December 10, 1949, an early contender for the first rock-and-roll record.

Louisiana connection: Live to disc on Rampart Street

Matassa later moved to magnetic tape and slowly added more tracks, but he always favored capturing the energy of New Orleans musicians playing together in one room, with as little fixing afterward as possible. His studio was designated a historic Rock and Roll Landmark in 2010.

    Step 4: Mixdown

    When every part is recorded and edited, it is time for mixdown: blending all the tracks into one finished stereo (or mono) file. The textbook lists five things the engineer shapes in a mix: relative level (how loud each track is), spatial position or panning (placing a sound left, center, or right), equalization or EQ (adjusting bass, middle, and treble), dynamics processing such as compression (evening out loud and soft moments), and effects such as reverb and delay.

    A typical mix starts with a rough balance of levels and panning. The producer listens and asks for changes, tracks are soloed (played alone) to fix their EQ, compression is added where needed, and reverb gives close-miked sounds a sense of space. In a DAW, automation records fader moves, such as raising the guitar during a solo, so the computer repeats them perfectly every time. The Berklee course reminds mixers that in a pop song, the lead vocal is the most important element, so build the mix to support it.

    Listening habits matter. The textbook recommends mixing at a moderate volume, about what you would hear at home or in a car, because our ears hear the balance of bass and treble differently at different volumes. When the mix is done, check it on many systems: earbuds, a phone speaker, a car, and big speakers. Also check it in mono, with both channels combined, to make sure nothing disappears.

    For podcasts, mixing means balancing each voice so no host is much louder than another, trimming silences and mistakes, fading the intro music under the first words, and exporting. The GarageBand video and Podcasting Pro Basics both suggest exporting spoken-word podcasts as MP3 files, since the difference from a full-quality WAV file is hard to hear for speech but the file is far smaller.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    Professional mixers take breaks. After hours of listening, ears get tired and every mix starts to sound fine.
    Many engineers play a favorite professional song next to their mix to compare the balance.

    Connection: Film editing

    A film editor cuts and orders scenes; a re-recording mixer then balances dialogue, music, and sound effects so the story is always clear. Mixing a song or podcast uses the same skills.

    Steps 5 to 8: Mastering to Release

    Mastering is the final polish. A mastering engineer adjusts the finished mixes so they sound their best and work together as a set: balancing the level between songs, fine-tuning EQ and dynamics across the whole track, and setting the overall loudness. MusicTech's beginner guide warns to be subtle and never master just to make a song louder; a great master is full and dynamic, with real highs and lows.

    Today, most streaming services measure loudness and turn very loud songs down to match quieter ones, so squashing a mix to win a "loudness war" no longer wins anything. Podcast hosts and platforms follow similar loudness guidelines so listeners do not have to keep adjusting the volume between episodes.

    Song sequence editing decides the order of the tracks on an album and the length of the silence between them. A short gap can make one song flow into the next if the moods match, and a longer gap gives listeners a moment to reset before something different. For a podcast season, the same thinking applies to the order of episodes and segments.

    Product manufacturing once meant pressing CDs and vinyl records and printing the artwork; the textbook advises asking for a proof copy before making thousands. Today most releases are digital: songs go to streaming services through a distributor, and podcasts are uploaded to a podcast host, which creates an RSS feed, a web address that automatically sends each new episode to apps like Apple Podcasts and Spotify.

    Fact check

    Podcasting Pro Basics (2016) tells readers to submit shows to "iTunes and Stitcher." iTunes podcasts are now Apple Podcasts, and Stitcher shut down in 2023. Today's main destinations include Apple Podcasts, Spotify, and YouTube.

    Finally, marketing and sales. The textbook calls it one of the most important steps and says to plan it before recording even starts: Who is my audience? How will they find the music? What should it look and sound like? For podcasts, the guides you uploaded add a consistent release schedule, eye-catching square cover art, and short video clips to help new listeners discover the show.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    The audio CD standard requires a two-second gap of silence at the very beginning of the first track.
    The textbook advises saving a backup copy of the final mixes and session files in case changes are needed years later.

    Connections: One Process, Many Arts

    The eight steps are not only for albums. A podcast, a film score, and a theatre soundtrack move through nearly the same path: plan, capture, add layers, blend, polish, order, deliver, and share. The table shows how the big ideas of this lesson look in each field.

    Connection: Theatre sound design

    In theatre, the "mix" happens live every night. A sound operator follows cues in the script, balancing actors' body mics and recorded effects in real time, which makes preparation even more important.
