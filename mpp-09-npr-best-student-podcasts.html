The Student Podcast Challenge

NPR, short for National Public Radio, is a nonprofit news organization that produces radio programs such as Morning Edition and All Things Considered, along with many podcasts. Its shows air on hundreds of local member stations across the country. In 2018, NPR's education team started the Student Podcast Challenge, a national contest that invites students to make a podcast and send it in.

The contest is open to students in grades four through 12. A teacher, educator, or other adult must submit each entry, but the podcast itself must be made by students: NPR reminds parents and teachers to "step away from the microphone." In 2026, its eighth year, NPR received about 1,500 podcasts from students in 42 states and the District of Columbia. By the end of 2024, the team had already listened to more than 17,000 student podcasts.

NPR's education team listens to every entry, then chooses finalists: in 2026, ten from middle school (grades five through eight) and ten from high school (grades nine through 12), plus honorable mentions. A panel of judges then picks a grand prize winner in each group. Winners are featured on NPR programs such as Morning Edition and All Things Considered, so millions of people hear a student's work.

This lesson looks closely at the 2026 winners and finalists to answer one question: what makes a student podcast one of the best in America? The answer, as you will see, has less to do with expensive equipment and more to do with curiosity, honesty, and careful editing.

High School Winner: "What Happens to Memories?"

The 2026 high school grand prize went to Amit Biligiri, a 16-year-old junior at Evergreen Valley High School in San Jose, California, for his podcast What Happens to Memories? The idea came from an ordinary argument at home: his mom said staying up late was hurting his memory. Amit wondered why he forgets so much of his daily life, yet still remembers one tiny moment from when he was six.

That moment became his hook. Amit opens by describing sharing French fries with his great-grandfather at a fast-food restaurant in India, both wearing paper crowns. He does not remember what they talked about, but he remembers the salt on the fries and the way the crown sat crooked on his great-grandfather's head. Those sensory details, specific things you can see, hear, taste, or feel, pull listeners into his memory.

From that personal question, Amit researched how memory works. He explains that during sleep the brain sorts through the day, keeping some memories and throwing others away, so his mom was right about sleep. He also argues that experiencing life through a phone screen leaves us only half-present, and that strong emotion is what helps memories stick.

Notice the structure. Amit moves from a personal story, to a question, to research, to a bigger idea, and then back to the personal. He ends by suggesting that memories may be proof that someone truly mattered. NPR reports that his mother now listens to the podcast on her own because it makes her feel closer to her grandfather. A personal story told well can reach the whole family, and the whole country.

You can read about Amit's podcast and hear it on NPR here: "Why we remember: One student's journey into memory".

Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

The podcast began with an argument about bedtime. Everyday disagreements can lead to great questions.
While making the podcast, Amit discovered an old photo of the fast-food moment that he had never seen before.

Connection: Visual art and memoir

Painters and memoir writers often build a whole work from one small, vivid moment. Amit's paper crown and salty fries work like a single detailed brushstroke that makes a whole scene come alive.

Middle School Winner: "Dyslexia and MEE"

The 2026 middle school grand prize went to Cora Walsh, a seventh grader from Long Island, New York, for Dyslexia and MEE. Dyslexia is a common learning difference that makes reading, spelling, and connecting letters to sounds harder, even for people who are very smart. Cora had always had a hard time reading, and her podcast tells the story of her nearly five-year journey to a correct diagnosis.

Cora did not just tell her own story; she interviewed the people closest to it. She talked with her mom and with her tutor to understand her diagnosis better, and she explored what life is like for adults with dyslexia. Her message is hopeful: she says people with dyslexia can be amazing in many ways, and her dyslexia is not all bad.

Her podcast also has a clear purpose beyond winning. NPR reports that Cora hopes her podcast helps make dyslexia feel less overwhelming for younger students who are still figuring out how they learn best. Knowing who you want to help is a powerful way to choose what to include and what to cut.

Cora's podcast shows something important: a winning topic does not have to be a big news story. It can be something you know from the inside. When a podcaster speaks honestly about her own experience, with care and a goal of helping others, listeners trust her and keep listening.

More Finalists, More Formats

The 2026 finalists show how many different shapes a great student podcast can take. In Why Collect?, high schooler Jay Burnett of Seattle asks why people collect things, and he finds answers by talking to friends and fellow collectors. Is collecting about the challenge, or about control? His podcast is an explainer built from interviews.

In Andy Warhol Was a Woman Named Patricia, middle schooler Ike Anolik of Brooklyn interviews writer Pat Hackett about her years working with the famous pop artist Andy Warhol. A playful, mysterious title makes listeners curious enough to press play.

A team of six students from Illini Bluffs High School in Glasford, Illinois, became finalists in their very first year entering. Their podcast, A Small Town's Meteoric Significance, explores the crater left when a meteor struck the area about 450 million years ago, and how it still affects life in their village of about 850 people. Their interest began in middle school, after their school was evacuated in 2021 because people smelled gas. It is a local history story that only they could tell.

And in Leaving Greece, middle schooler Livie Courser of Ohio interviews her grandfather about leaving his small village on the island of Crete for America. Family oral history, recording the memories of people who lived through events, appears again and again among the finalists, because every family holds stories that no textbook has.

    What the Judges Listen For

    NPR's published judging questions, used for its podcast challenges, focus on four things. Story: does the podcast tell a compelling story or teach us something new and important? Structure: is it organized in a way that makes sense and keeps listeners engaged? Clarity: can listeners easily follow the story or the information? Editing: did the students cut unnecessary information and repetition so the main ideas come through clearly?

    NPR also says the story matters as much as, or more than, audio production skills. In its words, the best podcasts are the ones where students bring a particular passion for the story they want to tell. Several finalists and past winners, including a recent high school grand prize winner, were first-time podcasters.

    The judges have warned about one common mistake: too many sound effects. NPR's editors noted that just because sound effects are fun and available does not mean you need them, and that when it comes to sound effects, the fewer the better. Use sound when it tells part of the story, such as a real recording of a place, not as decoration.

    Finally, rules matter. Entries that break the rules are disqualified, no matter how good they are. For the grades 4 to 12 challenge, podcasts must be between three and eight minutes long, must be original work by students, must keep language clean, and may only use music the students created themselves, because copyrighted music is not allowed.

    Themes and Past Winners

    Each year the entries reveal what is on young people's minds. In 2026, NPR heard many podcasts about technology in school, including AI, cellphone bans, and screen time, and many about memory: why some memories stick and others fade. NPR also offered a special prize marking the 250th anniversary of the United States, asking students what "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness" means to them.

    The 2025 winners show the same strengths. High school winner Avani Yaltho of Houston brought three generations of her family together to talk about their shared history and their family's village in Kerala, India. Middle school winner Ameya Desai, a fifth grader in San Jose, California, discovered that her hometown played a role in the incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II and interviewed a woman who had been sent to a camp as a child. Ameya became the contest's first returning champion.

    In 2025, NPR received nearly 2,000 entries from 45 states and the District of Columbia. Some were journalistic, investigating a wildfire's effect on a community. Others were creative: fifth graders made a funny podcast about Bigfoot with homemade sound effects, and a young musician in Cicero, Illinois, took listeners along as he composed a song.

    Across the years, a pattern appears. Winning podcasts start with a genuine question, use real voices (often family members), include research that goes beyond personal opinion, and end with an idea that matters to people other than the podcaster.

    Hotspots: tap a glowing light for a bonus fact

    The Cicero student's podcast shows that a podcast can be about music itself, a natural fit for a music production class.
    Ameya discovered national history in her own town. Ask older neighbors what they remember about your neighborhood.

    Connection: Music composition

    A student who composes his own song inside his podcast is doing two arts at once. Your own original music can serve as your theme, your transitions, or even your topic.

    Listening Like a Judge

    To learn from the best student podcasts, you need to listen actively. The Berklee music production course describes how producers listen: responding to a recording's emotional content without prejudice, reacting as though hearing it for the first time even on the hundredth listen, listening on many levels at once, and noting problems without letting that pull them out of the emotion of the piece. These critical listening skills work for podcasts too.

    The course recommends a routine. Find a quiet place free from distractions, ideally with good headphones, and silence your phone. Let go of your prejudices about the topic. Keep a blank page nearby and jot short notes about what you feel while you listen. Immediately afterward, expand those notes while the feeling is still fresh.

    For a podcast, listen in layers. On the first listen, notice how you feel: curious, moved, bored, confused? On the second, map the structure: where does the hook end, where are the sections, how does it close? On the third, focus on sound: are the voices clear, are the levels even, is music or natural sound used with purpose?

    Your notes become a checklist for your own work. If a finalist hooked you with a sensory detail, try one in your intro. If a long pause lost your attention, edit your own pauses more tightly. Studying models closely, then trying their moves, is exactly how the webinar in Lesson 6 described using mentor texts.
